Lids and adnexa

Lid lesions: histology drives the diagnosis
LesionHistologyClinical key
ChalazionLipogranuloma: zonal granulomatous inflammation around extravasated meibum with lipid vacuoles and giant cellsPainless nodule; recurrent or atypical lesions in the elderly must be biopsied.
Seborrhoeic keratosisAcanthosis, hyperkeratosis, and pseudo-horn cysts of keratinGreasy, stuck-on, benign.
Actinic keratosisEpidermal dysplasia with parakeratosis and solar elastosisPremalignant; scaly, sun-exposed skin.
KeratoacanthomaRapidly growing cup-shaped lesion with a keratin-filled crater; well-differentiated squamous proliferationConsidered a low-grade squamous cell carcinoma variant; grows over weeks.
Basal cell carcinomaNests of basaloid cells with peripheral palisading and retraction artefact; nodular, morpheaform, or superficial 85–90% of lid malignancies; commonest on the lower lid and medial canthus. Pearly rolled edge with telangiectasia and central ulceration. Locally invasive, rarely metastasises; medial canthal morpheaform lesions are the most dangerous for orbital invasion.
Squamous cell carcinomaAtypical keratinocytes with keratin pearls, invading the dermis; may show perineural invasion Second commonest; more aggressive with true metastatic potential and perineural spread to the orbit and cavernous sinus.
Sebaceous cell carcinomaLobules of atypical cells with foamy, lipid-laden cytoplasm; oil-red-O positive on fresh tissue; pagetoid spread through the epithelium; multicentric The great masquerader. Presents as a recurrent chalazion, or as a unilateral chronic "blepharoconjunctivitis" that fails to respond. Favours the upper lid (more meibomian glands). Loss of lashes (madarosis) is a red flag. Associated with Muir-Torre syndrome. High mortality if missed.
MelanomaAtypical melanocytes; Breslow depth and mitotic rate drive prognosisRare on the lid; consider in any pigmented lesion with ABCDE features. Lentigo maligna in sun-damaged skin.
Merkel cell carcinomaSmall blue cells with neuroendocrine markers (CK20 positive, dot-like perinuclear pattern)Violaceous, rapidly growing; aggressive with early nodal metastasis; Merkel cell polyomavirus association.
XanthelasmaDermal foamy lipid-laden histiocytesMedial lids; check the lipid profile, especially in the young.
Molluscum contagiosumLobular epidermal hyperplasia with Henderson-Paterson intracytoplasmic inclusion bodiesUmbilicated nodule; multiple lesions suggest HIV.
Two lid malignancy rules

Any "recurrent chalazion", especially in the upper lid of a patient over 50, must be biopsied to exclude sebaceous cell carcinoma. Similarly, a unilateral chronic blepharoconjunctivitis unresponsive to treatment is a sebaceous carcinoma until proven otherwise. Warn the pathologist to keep fresh tissue for lipid stains, because routine processing dissolves the lipid.

Madarosis, loss of lid margin architecture, telangiectasia, ulceration, induration, and distortion of the punctum are the six features that convert a benign-looking lesion into a biopsy.

Conjunctiva and cornea

Conjunctival pathology
LesionHistology and significance
PingueculaSubepithelial elastotic degeneration of collagen with basophilic hyaline change; no corneal involvement.
PterygiumSame elastotic degeneration plus destruction of Bowman layer and fibrovascular ingrowth onto the cornea; a Stocker line of iron marks the advancing edge.
Conjunctival naevusNests of benign melanocytes, often with intralesional cysts; almost never involves the palpebral conjunctiva or fornix (a lesion there is suspicious).

Uveal melanoma: the histology that predicts survival
  • Cell type (modified Callender): spindle cells have the best prognosis; mixed intermediate; epithelioid the worst.
  • Size is the strongest clinical predictor: largest basal diameter and thickness. Thickness above 2 mm, subretinal fluid, symptoms, orange pigment (lipofuscin), margin within 3 mm of the disc, and absence of drusen or a halo distinguish a small melanoma from a naevus ("To Find Small Ocular Melanoma Doing IMaging").
  • Extrascleral extension, vortex vein invasion, and high mitotic rate worsen prognosis.
  • Monosomy 3 and 8q gain, and class 2 gene expression profile, predict metastasis. GNAQ/GNA11 mutations are near-universal drivers; BAP1 loss is a strong adverse marker; EIF1AX and SF3B1 mutations are more favourable.
  • Metastasis is haematogenous and overwhelmingly to the liver, because the eye has no lymphatics. Screen with liver imaging and enzymes.
  • Mushroom (collar-stud) shape on B-scan with low internal reflectivity and choroidal excavation is characteristic; the shape results from rupture through Bruch membrane.
Retinoblastoma and other intraocular tumours
TumourHistology and features
Retinoblastoma Small round blue cells with scant cytoplasm, high mitotic rate, necrosis with calcification (the finding that makes CT and ultrasound diagnostic), and Flexner-Wintersteiner rosettes (a single layer of cells around a true lumen, indicating photoreceptor differentiation). Homer Wright rosettes (no lumen) and fleurettes also occur. RB1 tumour suppressor at 13q14, following the two-hit hypothesis; heritable disease is bilateral, multifocal, earlier, and carries a risk of trilateral retinoblastoma (pineoblastoma) and second malignancies, especially osteosarcoma. Leukocoria and strabismus are the presenting signs. Optic nerve invasion beyond the lamina, choroidal invasion, and extrascleral extension are the key adverse pathologic features.
Choroidal metastasisThe commonest intraocular malignancy overall. Creamy, plateau-shaped, often multifocal and bilateral, with high internal reflectivity. Breast in women, lung in men.
Choroidal haemangiomaCavernous vascular channels; orange-red, high internal reflectivity, early intense fluorescence with late washout. Diffuse form in Sturge-Weber.
Choroidal osteomaMature bone in the choroid; juxtapapillary, orange-yellow, dense acoustic shadowing on B-scan.
Astrocytic hamartomaGlial proliferation; mulberry calcified lesion in tuberous sclerosis (TSC1, TSC2).
Retinal capillary haemangioblastomaVon Hippel-Lindau (VHL, 3p25); red globe with dilated feeder vessels and exudation; screen for renal cell carcinoma and phaeochromocytoma.
Combined hamartoma of retina and RPEGlial, vascular, and pigmented proliferation with retinal traction; associated with neurofibromatosis 2.
Primary vitreoretinal lymphomaLarge atypical B cells, subretinal pigment epithelial infiltration; vitreous IL-10 exceeding IL-6, and MYD88 mutation. A masquerade for chronic uveitis in the elderly; requires CNS imaging.

Retina, optic nerve, and glaucoma

Histologic sequence of diabetic retinopathy
  1. Capillary basement membrane thickening and loss of pericytes.
  2. Microaneurysms (saccular outpouchings, best seen on angiography), and increased permeability with leakage of plasma into the retina.
  3. Intraretinal haemorrhage: dot-and-blot in the deeper layers, flame-shaped in the nerve fibre layer.
  4. Hard exudates: lipoprotein and lipid-laden macrophages, deposited at the junction of oedematous and normal retina, hence their circinate arrangement.
  5. Capillary closure with acellular capillaries, cotton wool spots (nerve fibre layer infarcts with cytoid bodies representing swollen axonal end bulbs), intraretinal microvascular abnormalities, and venous beading.
  6. Ischaemia-driven VEGF release → neovascularisation at the disc and elsewhere, growing on the retinal surface and along the posterior hyaloid.
  7. Fibrovascular proliferation, vitreous haemorrhage, tractional retinal detachment, and finally iris neovascularisation with neovascular glaucoma.
Retinal and vascular pathology patterns
EntityPathology
Cotton wool spotFocal ischaemic infarct of the nerve fibre layer with cytoid bodies: swollen axonal end bulbs with pseudonuclei.
Cystoid macular oedemaFluid accumulation predominantly in the inner nuclear and outer plexiform (Henle) layers, producing a petalloid angiographic pattern. Causes: post-cataract surgery (Irvine-Gass), diabetes, vein occlusion, uveitis, epinephrine and prostaglandin analogues, retinitis pigmentosa, nicotinic acid (which does not leak).
DrusenFocal deposits between the RPE basal lamina and Bruch membrane. Hard (small, discrete) versus soft (large, confluent, higher AMD risk). Distinguish from optic disc drusen, which are calcified hyaline bodies anterior to the lamina cribrosa causing pseudopapilloedema and autofluorescence.
Retinitis pigmentosaProgressive rod then cone loss with RPE migration into the retina ("bone spicule" pigment), arteriolar attenuation, waxy disc pallor, and posterior subcapsular cataract. Rod-cone ERG reduced early.
Age-related macular degenerationNon-exudative: drusen, RPE change, and geographic atrophy with photoreceptor and choriocapillaris loss. Exudative: choroidal neovascularisation through Bruch membrane, with pigment epithelial detachment, subretinal haemorrhage, and eventual disciform fibrovascular scar.
Retinal detachmentSeparation at the subretinal space, that is between photoreceptors and RPE, not between retina and choroid. Rhegmatogenous (break plus liquefied vitreous plus traction), tractional, or exudative. Chronic detachments show demarcation lines, intraretinal cysts, and subretinal precipitates.
Retinal artery occlusionCoagulative necrosis of the inner retina with inner nuclear layer and ganglion cell loss; the outer retina survives on choroidal supply.
Glaucomatous optic neuropathy Loss of retinal ganglion cell axons with posterior bowing and remodelling of the lamina cribrosa, enlargement of the cup, thinning of the neuroretinal rim (worst at the inferior and superior poles), laminar dot sign, bayoneting and nasalisation of vessels, and Schnabel cavernous degeneration retrolaminarly. Ganglion cells die by apoptosis, and there is no inflammation.

Trauma pathology

Mechanisms and named findings in ocular trauma
InjuryPathology
Blunt (contusion) injuryAnteroposterior compression with equatorial expansion. Produces, from front to back: corneal abrasion, hyphaema, iridodialysis, sphincter tears (traumatic mydriasis), angle recession, cyclodialysis, lens subluxation, Vossius ring (imprinted iris pigment on the anterior capsule), rosette cataract, retinal dialysis (classically superonasal or inferotemporal), commotio retinae (photoreceptor outer segment disruption, not oedema), choroidal rupture, and sclopetaria.
Chemical burn Alkali saponifies membrane lipids and penetrates rapidly, causing deep and progressive damage with limbal ischaemia and late glaucoma; acid coagulates proteins, which limits penetration (hydrofluoric acid excepted). Limbal blanching extent is the prognostic sign (Roper-Hall and Dua classifications). Copious irrigation until pH normalises comes before everything else.
Intraocular foreign bodySiderosis (iron: heterochromia, mydriasis, cataract, pigmentary retinopathy, reduced ERG) and chalcosis (copper: Kayser-Fleischer-like ring, sunflower cataract; pure copper causes suppurative endophthalmitis). Inert materials include glass, plastic, and gold.
Sympathetic ophthalmiaBilateral diffuse granulomatous panuveitis sparing the choriocapillaris and retina, with Dalen-Fuchs nodules (epithelioid histiocytes between RPE and Bruch). Latency two weeks to decades, most within three months. Prevention is prompt repair or, rarely, enucleation of a blind injured eye.
Shaken baby syndromeMultilayered retinal haemorrhages extending to the periphery, retinoschisis with a perimacular fold, and vitreous haemorrhage, with subdural haematoma and no external signs. A mandatory-report finding.
Purtscher retinopathyMultiple polygonal areas of retinal whitening (Purtscher flecken) after chest compression, long bone fracture, or acute pancreatitis; complement-mediated leucoembolisation.
Solar and laser retinopathyPhotochemical (solar) or photothermal/photodisruptive (laser) foveal damage with a characteristic outer retinal defect on OCT.

Key
  1. Lattice: amyloid, Congo red with apple-green birefringence. Granular: hyaline, Masson trichrome. Macular: keratan sulfate mucopolysaccharide, Alcian blue and colloidal iron.
  2. Macular dystrophy; the intervening stroma is hazy and the opacity extends to the limbus, and the cornea is thin.
  3. Sebaceous cell carcinoma; oil-red-O on fresh (unfixed) tissue; warn the pathologist to preserve fresh tissue because routine processing dissolves lipid, and request evaluation for pagetoid spread.
  4. Langhans giant cells in tuberculosis; Touton giant cells in juvenile xanthogranuloma.
  5. Epithelioid; monosomy 3, 8q gain, class 2 gene expression profile, and BAP1 loss.
  6. To the liver, haematogenously, because the eye has no lymphatic drainage.
  7. A single layer of tumour cells arranged around a true central lumen, indicating photoreceptor differentiation; characteristic of retinoblastoma.
  8. RB1 at 13q14; two-hit hypothesis with a germline first hit in heritable disease; pineoblastoma (trilateral retinoblastoma) and osteosarcoma.
  9. Capillary basement membrane thickening and pericyte loss, then microaneurysms with increased permeability, then intraretinal haemorrhage.
  10. A swollen axonal end bulb with a pseudonucleus, found in a cotton wool spot.
  11. Inner nuclear and outer plexiform (Henle) layers; nicotinic acid (niacin) maculopathy does not leak on fluorescein.
  12. Alkali saponifies lipids and penetrates deeply and progressively; acid coagulates protein, which limits penetration. The extent of limbal (perilimbal) blanching is the best prognostic sign.
  13. Dalen-Fuchs nodules, epithelioid histiocytes between the RPE and Bruch membrane; latency two weeks to decades, most within three months.
  14. Disruption of photoreceptor outer segments, not true retinal oedema.
Chapter recap
  1. Five adaptations, two death modes; apoptosis is the mode in glaucoma and retinitis pigmentosa.
  2. Granulomatous means epithelioid histiocytes and giant cells, and clinically means mutton-fat precipitates and iris nodules.
  3. BCC is the commonest lid malignancy, lower lid and medial canthus; sebaceous carcinoma masquerades as chalazion or blepharitis on the upper lid.
  4. Marilyn Monroe: macular-mucopolysaccharide-Alcian, granular-hyaline-Masson, lattice-amyloid-Congo.
  5. Macular dystrophy is the recessive one, with hazy intervening stroma and a thin cornea.
  6. Melanoma: epithelioid, large, monosomy 3, BAP1 loss, class 2 profile; liver metastasis.
  7. Retinoblastoma: RB1 13q14, two hits, calcification, Flexner-Wintersteiner rosettes, trilateral disease.
  8. Diabetic retinopathy starts with pericyte loss and ends with VEGF-driven fibrovascular traction.
  9. Glaucoma is apoptotic ganglion cell loss with laminar remodelling and no inflammation.
  10. Blunt trauma has a named finding at every anteroposterior level; alkali burns are prognosticated by limbal blanching.
