Theories of colour vision

Three levels of explanation, all correct at their own level
TheoryClaimEvidence and level of processing
Trichromatic (Young-Helmholtz)Three cone classes with overlapping broad spectral sensitivities; colour is coded by the ratio of their responses Correct at the receptor level. Explains why three primaries can match any colour and why metamers exist (physically different spectra producing identical cone ratios).
Opponent process (Hering)Colour is coded in antagonistic pairs: red-green, blue-yellow, and black-white Correct at the post-receptoral level (horizontal cells, bipolar cells, ganglion cells, LGN). Explains why there is no reddish-green, why negative afterimages are complementary, and why simultaneous colour contrast occurs.
Zone (stage) theoryTrichromatic at the receptors, then recoded into opponent channelsThe synthesis, and the answer to any item asking which theory is "correct".
  • Cone peak sensitivities: S (blue) ~419–440 nm, M (green) ~531–535 nm, L (red) ~558–565 nm. Rod (rhodopsin) peak ~496–500 nm.
  • Photopic luminous efficiency peaks at 555 nm; scotopic at 507 nm (the Purkinje shift).
  • Cone proportions: roughly L : M : S = 10 : 5 : 1, with S cones only about 5–10% and absent from the very centre of the foveola (small-field tritanopia).
  • Colour appearance phenomena: the Bezold-Brücke shift (hue changes with intensity), the Abney effect (hue changes with desaturation), the Helmholtz-Kohlrausch effect (saturated colours look brighter), and colour constancy (perceived colour remains stable across illuminants).

Prevalence numbers and the nomenclature system

Red-green defects affect about 8% of males and 0.5% of females. Because the L and M opsin genes lie in a tandem array on Xq28, unequal crossing over between the highly homologous genes is the mechanism, and it explains both the X-linked pattern and why anomalous (shifted) pigments are commoner than absent ones. The S opsin gene is on chromosome 7, hence autosomal dominant tritan defects.

Suffix rules: -anomaly = anomalous trichromat (three pigments, one shifted); -anopia = dichromat (one pigment missing); monochromacy = one or no functioning cone class. Prot- refers to the first (long-wavelength, L) pigment, deuter- the second (M), trit- the third (S).

Acquired colour vision defects

Köllner rule, and the exceptions that get tested

Outer retinal (photoreceptor and RPE) disease tends to cause blue-yellow defects; optic nerve disease tends to cause red-green defects.

ExceptionDirection
GlaucomaOptic nerve disease, but produces an early blue-yellow loss (the basis of short-wavelength automated perimetry).
Autosomal dominant optic atrophy (OPA1)Optic nerve, but classically blue-yellow (tritan).
Stargardt disease and cone dystrophiesOuter retina, but often red-green.
Chloroquine and hydroxychloroquine maculopathyOuter retina, and can present with red-green errors.

Other characteristics of acquired defects: they are frequently asymmetric between the eyes, are often accompanied by reduced acuity, may be described in qualitative terms ("colours look washed out"), and change over time. Congenital defects are symmetric, stable, and usually have normal acuity. Asymmetry between eyes is the single best clue that a colour defect is acquired.

Ocular and systemic causes to know: optic neuritis and other optic neuropathies (red desaturation is an early and sensitive sign), nutritional and toxic optic neuropathy (ethambutol, methanol, tobacco-alcohol), digoxin (xanthopsia, yellow vision), sildenafil (transient blue tinge via PDE6), amiodarone, and jaundice or cataract producing a generalised blue-yellow shift by absorption.

Perceptual function

Visual acuity: four kinds

Acuity types and their thresholds
TypeThresholdTask
Detection (minimum visible)~0.5 arcsec for a bright line on a dark fieldIs anything there? Depends on contrast, not on receptor spacing.
Resolution (minimum separable)~30–60 arcsec (0.5–1 arcmin)Are these two things separate? Limited by cone spacing (the Nyquist limit) and by diffraction. Grating and Landolt C acuity.
Recognition~1 arcmin per stroke width, so a 20/20 letter subtends 5 arcminWhat is it? Snellen and logMAR charts. Involves cognitive identification, so it is affected by crowding and literacy.
Vernier (hyperacuity)~2–5 arcsecAre these lines aligned? Far finer than cone spacing, because it relies on comparing the centroid of a distribution across many receptors: hence "hyperacuity". Also underlies stereoacuity.
  • MAR and logMAR: MAR is the minimum angle of resolution in arcmin; logMAR = log10(MAR). 20/20 = MAR 1 = logMAR 0.00. 20/200 = MAR 10 = logMAR 1.00. 20/40 = MAR 2 = logMAR 0.30. Each line on a logMAR chart is 0.1 log units, with 5 letters per line so each letter is worth 0.02.
  • Advantages of logMAR (Bailey-Lovie, ETDRS) over Snellen: equal letter count per line, logarithmic progression of size, constant inter-letter and inter-line spacing proportional to letter size, and equal legibility of letters. This makes acuity a genuine interval scale suitable for statistics, which is why every clinical trial uses it.
  • Pinhole acuity improves if blur is refractive or from irregular optics; it does not improve, and often worsens, with retinal or neural disease and in a small pupil or media opacity.

Contrast sensitivity

  • Contrast sensitivity = 1 / contrast threshold. Plotted against spatial frequency it gives the contrast sensitivity function, an inverted U peaking at 3–6 cycles per degree and falling to zero at about 30–60 cpd (the acuity limit). The low-frequency fall-off is caused by lateral inhibition.
  • Acuity measures only the high-frequency cut-off. Disease can devastate mid-frequency sensitivity while leaving acuity intact: this is the pattern in optic neuritis, glaucoma, amblyopia, early cataract, and after refractive surgery, and it explains patients with 20/20 acuity who complain that vision is not right.
  • Tests: Pelli-Robson (letter, at a fixed spatial frequency), Vistech and FACT (sine-wave gratings at several frequencies), Mars letter chart, and low-contrast logMAR charts (2.5% and 1.25%).
  • Glare: discomfort glare is a sensation; disability glare is measurable loss of contrast sensitivity in the presence of a glare source, quantified by brightness acuity testing. Both are prominent in cataract and in corneal oedema and scatter.

Psychophysical laws worth naming

Named relationships in visual psychophysics
LawStatement
Weber lawThe just-noticeable difference is a constant fraction of the background: ΔI / I = k. Underlies contrast constancy across illumination levels.
Fechner lawPerceived magnitude is proportional to the logarithm of stimulus intensity. Why perimetric sensitivity is expressed in decibels.
Ricco lawFor small stimuli, threshold intensity × area = constant: complete spatial summation. Applies below a critical area that grows with eccentricity.
Piper lawFor intermediate areas, threshold × √area = constant: partial summation.
Bloch lawFor brief stimuli, threshold intensity × duration = constant: complete temporal summation, up to a critical duration of about 100 ms.
Ferry-Porter lawCritical flicker fusion frequency rises linearly with the logarithm of retinal illuminance. CFF is about 50–60 Hz photopically and much lower scotopically, and it is reduced in optic nerve disease.
Talbot-Plateau lawAbove the fusion frequency, a flickering light appears as steady light of the time-averaged luminance.
Broca-Sulzer effectA brief flash of intermediate duration appears brighter than a longer one of the same luminance.
Stiles-Crawford effectCones are most sensitive to light entering through the pupil centre; a waveguide directional effect.
Troxler fadingA steadily fixated peripheral stimulus fades from awareness; the basis of the disappearance of stabilised images and of some entoptic phenomena.

Entoptic phenomena and illusions

  • Haidinger brushes: a yellow propeller pattern seen in polarised blue light, generated by the radially arranged macular xanthophyll and Henle fibres. Used to demonstrate and train foveal fixation.
  • Maxwell spot: a dark central spot seen against certain colours, again from macular pigment absorption.
  • Purkinje tree: shadows of retinal vessels seen with a moving light source; used to assess retinal function through dense media opacity.
  • Blue field entoptoscope: white cells moving in perifoveal capillaries, used to assess macular perfusion.
  • Phosphenes: pressure or mechanically induced light perception, and the basis of the Amsler pressure-phosphene test in dense media opacity.
  • Mach bands and the Hermann grid: direct behavioural evidence of lateral inhibition.
  • Gestalt grouping principles: proximity, similarity, continuity, closure, common fate, figure-ground. These explain contour completion, why a scotoma can be "filled in", and the perceptual completion in hemianopia.

The visual field

  • Normal monocular extent: temporally 90–100°, nasally ~60°, superiorly ~60°, inferiorly ~70–75°. The binocular field spans about 200° horizontally with a central 120° of overlap; the monocular temporal crescent occupies the outer 60–90°.
  • Physiologic blind spot: centred about 15° temporal to fixation and 1.5–2° below the horizontal, measuring roughly 5° wide by 7° tall.
  • Traquair island of vision: the classic metaphor of a hill of vision in a sea of blindness. Sensitivity is highest at fixation and declines with eccentricity, and the whole hill sinks with age and with media opacity.
Perimetric indices and reliability
IndexMeaning and interpretation
Mean deviation (MD)Average departure from age-matched normal across the field. Sensitive to diffuse loss, so it is dragged down by cataract and small pupils as well as by disease.
Pattern standard deviation (PSD)Irregularity of the hill of vision. Sensitive to localised loss. It paradoxically improves in very advanced, uniformly depressed fields, so a low PSD with a very negative MD is not reassurance.
Visual field index (VFI)A percentage of age-normal function weighted toward central points, designed for rate-of-progression analysis and less affected by cataract than MD.
Glaucoma hemifield test (GHT)Compares mirror-image zones above and below the horizontal, exploiting the fact that glaucomatous loss respects the raphe. Reported as within normal limits, borderline, or outside normal limits.
Fixation lossesBlind spot catch trials. >20% is unreliable; may also indicate a mis-mapped blind spot.
False positivesResponse with no stimulus ("trigger happy"). >15% is unreliable, and it produces spuriously good, sometimes supranormal, sensitivities with a white scotoma pattern.
False negativesFailure to respond to a much brighter stimulus in a seeing area. >33% suggests inattention, but is also common in genuinely advanced disease, where it should not be read as unreliability.
Short-term fluctuationRepeat testing of selected points; rises in early disease and in inexperienced patients.
Artefacts that mimic disease
  • Lens rim artefact: a dense, sharply demarcated peripheral arc, usually superior or all around; caused by the trial lens being too far from the eye or decentred.
  • Ptosis or brow: superior arcuate depression that respects the lid position rather than the raphe; tape the lid and repeat.
  • Learning effect: the first field is often worse than the second; never diagnose progression on two fields alone.
  • Cloverleaf pattern: sensitivity preserved only in the four zones tested first; indicates the patient stopped responding.
  • Small pupil or miotic: generalised depression with a worsened MD.
  • Uncorrected refractive error: generalised depression, worse centrally, and a high-plus trial lens can also create a rim artefact.
  • Spiral or tunnel fields with normal structure suggest non-organic visual loss; a true field defect enlarges appropriately with distance, whereas a functional tunnel does not.

Key
  1. Trichromatic (Young-Helmholtz) explains metamers; opponent process (Hering) explains complementary negative afterimages.
  2. Red-green: L and M opsins in a tandem array at Xq28, X-linked recessive. Blue-yellow: S opsin on chromosome 7, autosomal dominant.
  3. Deuteranomaly, about 5% of males.
  4. Congenital; the decisive feature is symmetry between the eyes (with stability and normal acuity supporting it).
  5. Outer retinal disease gives blue-yellow, optic nerve disease gives red-green. Exceptions: glaucoma (blue-yellow despite being optic nerve), dominant optic atrophy (blue-yellow), and Stargardt or cone dystrophy (red-green despite being outer retinal).
  6. Farnsworth-Munsell 100-hue for quantification; Farnsworth D-15 or desaturated Lanthony D-15 for rapid axis classification (HRR also classifies).
  7. The plate designs use confusion lines along the red-green axes only; there are no tritan confusion plates in the standard set.
  8. MAR = 50/20 = 2.5 arcmin; logMAR = log10(2.5) = 0.40.
  9. Snellen acuity measures only the high spatial frequency cut-off; optic neuritis characteristically depresses mid-frequency contrast sensitivity, which carries most real-world visual information.
  10. Ricco: below a critical area, threshold intensity × area is constant (complete spatial summation). Bloch: below a critical duration of about 100 ms, threshold intensity × duration is constant (complete temporal summation).
  11. A yellow propeller-shaped entoptic pattern seen in rotating polarised blue light, generated by macular xanthophyll along the radially arranged Henle fibres; used to demonstrate and train foveal fixation.
  12. No. PSD falls in advanced, uniformly depressed fields because there is little remaining irregularity. Interpret with MD and the VFI, and look at the pattern deviation plot.
  13. Lens rim artefact and ptosis or brow overhang. Reposition the lens, tape the lid, and repeat.
  14. Temporal 90 to 100°, nasal about 60°, superior about 60°, inferior about 70 to 75°; blind spot about 15° temporal and 1.5 to 2° below the horizontal, roughly 5 by 7 degrees.
Chapter recap
  1. Zone theory: trichromatic receptors feeding opponent channels.
  2. L and M at Xq28 X-linked; S on chromosome 7 autosomal dominant. 8% of males, 0.5% of females.
  3. Deuteranomaly is commonest; rod monochromacy gives a flat photopic ERG with a normal scotopic ERG.
  4. Köllner rule with its four exceptions; asymmetry between eyes means acquired.
  5. Ishihara screens congenital red-green only; HRR classifies both; 100-hue quantifies; anomaloscope is the reference.
  6. Four acuities: detection, resolution, recognition, and vernier hyperacuity at 2 to 5 arcsec.
  7. logMAR = log(MAR); 20/20 is logMAR 0, and each letter is 0.02.
  8. The CSF peaks at 3 to 6 cpd; acuity samples only its high-frequency edge.
  9. Weber, Fechner, Ricco, Piper, Bloch, Ferry-Porter, Talbot-Plateau: know the statement, not the derivation.
  10. MD for diffuse loss, PSD for focal loss, GHT for hemifield asymmetry, and always rule out lens rim and ptosis artefacts.
