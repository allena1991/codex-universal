Classifying ametropia

Myopia and hyperopia by mechanism
MechanismMyopiaHyperopia
AxialLong eye. The commonest form. Each 1 mm of excess length is about 3 D. Pathologic myopia above 26–26.5 mm. Short eye: nanophthalmos (high hyperopia, thick sclera, crowded angle, uveal effusion risk), microphthalmos, retinopathy of prematurity in some cases.
CurvatureSteep cornea or lens: keratoconus, keratoglobus, congenital microcornea with steep curvature, post-keratoplasty steepening. Flat cornea: cornea plana, post-hyperopic overcorrection, flat corneal grafts.
IndexNuclear sclerosis (the classic myopic shift and "second sight"), acute hyperglycaemia, sorbitol accumulation. Cortical cataract in some cases; treated diabetes with falling glucose can give a transient hyperopic shift.
PositionalAnterior lens displacement (anterior lens subluxation, ciliary body swelling, plateau iris configuration, drug-induced ciliary oedema). Posterior lens displacement, and aphakia (about +10 to +12 D at the spectacle plane).
Retinal / other–Anything that elevates the retina forward: central serous chorioretinopathy, choroidal tumour, macular oedema, posterior scleritis.
Pharmacologic and functional Pseudomyopia from accommodative spasm; miotics; sulfonamides, topiramate, acetazolamide, and tetracyclines cause acute myopia with ciliochoroidal effusion and angle narrowing. Night myopia from tonic accommodation plus spherical aberration and the Purkinje shift. Cycloplegics reveal latent hyperopia; systemic anticholinergics reduce accommodation and unmask it symptomatically.
Hyperopia terminology, in one relationship

Total hyperopia = latent + manifest, and manifest = facultative + absolute.

  • Total: revealed only under full cycloplegia.
  • Latent: masked by ciliary tonus; the difference between the cycloplegic and the manifest refraction.
  • Manifest: what you find on a non-cycloplegic maximum-plus refraction.
  • Facultative: the part the patient can overcome with accommodation (vision is normal with or without the plus lens).
  • Absolute: the part that cannot be overcome (vision is subnormal without correction).

Clinically this matters because a young hyperope's symptoms track the facultative component and their acuity tracks the absolute component. It is also why a child with accommodative esotropia needs a cycloplegic refraction: the latent component is what drives the deviation.

Anisometropia and aniseikonia

  • Anisometropia: a difference in refractive error between eyes. Aniseikonia: a difference in perceived retinal image size. As a rough rule, 1 D of spectacle anisometropia produces about 1–2% aniseikonia, and most patients tolerate up to about 3–5%.
  • Knapp law: if the ametropia is purely axial, correcting it with a lens whose secondary focal point coincides with the eye's anterior focal point (about 15.7 mm in front of the cornea, i.e. approximately the spectacle plane) yields equal retinal image sizes in the two eyes. For purely refractive anisometropia, a contact lens minimises the size difference.
  • Why Knapp law often fails clinically: in axial myopia the retina is also stretched, so photoreceptor density per degree falls. The theoretically equal image now falls on fewer receptors, and many axial anisometropes actually do better with contact lenses despite the prediction. Treat Knapp law as a starting point, not a rule to be applied blindly.
  • Unequal accommodative demand: correcting a myope with spectacles requires less accommodation at near than a contact lens does; correcting a hyperope with spectacles requires more. A −10 D myope switching from glasses to contact lenses can suddenly become symptomatic at near for exactly this reason, and this is a common presbyopia-onset complaint.
  • Unequal prismatic effect at near: in vertical anisometropia, reading below the optical centres induces unequal vertical prism (Prentice rule). Above about 1.5–2 Δ of vertical imbalance, options are slab-off (bicentric grinding of the more minus / less plus lens to add base-up at the reading level), reverse slab on the other lens, contact lenses, dissimilar segment heights, or a single-vision near pair.

Spectacle magnification

Spectacle magnification: two factors

SM = [ 1 / (1 − h Fv) ] × [ 1 / (1 − (t/n) F1) ]

FactorExpressionWhat changes it
Power factor1 / (1 − h Fv) h is the distance from the back vertex to the entrance pupil (about 3 mm behind the cornea, so h ≈ vertex distance + 3 mm). Plus lenses magnify, minus lenses minify, and reducing vertex distance reduces the magnification effect.
Shape factor1 / (1 − (t/n) F1) t is centre thickness, F1 the front surface power. Flatter front curves and thinner centres reduce magnification. This is the lever the optician controls without changing the prescription.

Practical consequences. A myope in spectacles sees a smaller world; a hyperope a larger one. Aphakic spectacles magnify by roughly 20–35%, which is why unilateral aphakia cannot be corrected with spectacles: the aniseikonia is intolerable and fusion is impossible. To reduce aniseikonia in a fixed prescription: reduce the thickness, flatten the base curve, and shorten the vertex distance of the more plus lens (or do the reverse on the other lens).

Ophthalmic lens design

Lens forms and when to use them
FormDescription and indication
Meniscus / periscopicOne convex and one concave surface; the standard modern form.
Best form (corrected curve)Base curves chosen from the Tscherning ellipse to eliminate oblique astigmatism or power error. Two solutions exist for each power; the flatter (Ostwalt) branch is used because the steep (Wollaston) branch is cosmetically unacceptable.

Vogel rule of thumbFront base curve ≈ spherical equivalent / 2 + 6 for plus lenses; for minus lenses, base curve ≈ spherical equivalent / 2 + 6 as well, treated as the flattest reasonable base. Base curve is measured on the front surface with a lens clock (a spherometer using the sag principle).
The sag formula, which underlies thickness, base curves, and contact lens fitting

s = y2 / 2r  or, in terms of surface power,   s = y2 F / [ 2000 (n − 1) ]

where s is sagittal depth, y the semi-diameter, and r the radius of curvature (consistent units). Steeper radius or larger diameter means greater sag. This one relationship explains lens thickness calculations, lens clock readings, why a larger contact lens diameter behaves as though it were fitted steeper, and the sagittal relationship in every soft lens fitting decision.

Centre thickness of a plus lens ≈ edge thickness + sag of front surface − sag of back surface. Minimum blank size = effective diameter + 2 × (total decentration) + 2 mm allowance. Total decentration per lens = (frame PD − patient PD) / 2, where frame PD = eye size + DBL.

Multifocals

Bifocal types: jump, displacement, and use
TypeOptical centre of segmentImage jump and clinical note
Round top (Kryptok, 22 mm)At the segment centre, well below the top Maximum image jump. Jump = (distance from seg top to seg optical centre in cm) × add. For a 22 mm round seg the centre is 11 mm below the top, so a +2.50 add gives 1.1 × 2.50 = 2.75 Δ of jump. Base-down jump; the image appears to leap upward as the eye crosses the line.

Trifocal (7×28, 8×35)Intermediate segment carries half the add (occasionally 40% or 70%) Two lines, two jumps. Indicated when the add exceeds about +1.75 to +2.00 D and intermediate range matters.

Jump versus displacement: two different quantities

Image jump is the sudden prismatic change as the line of sight crosses the segment top, and depends only on the segment design and add power, not on the distance prescription.

Total prismatic displacement at the reading level is the sum of the distance lens prism (Prentice rule at that point) and the segment prism. It does depend on the distance prescription. A myope reading below the optical centre gets base-up from the distance lens; a hyperope gets base-down. To minimise total displacement, pair a round-top segment with a myopic distance prescription and a flat-top segment with a hyperopic one, even though this is the opposite of what minimising jump alone would suggest.

Progressive lens fitting

  • Fitting cross is aligned with the pupil centre in habitual posture, not with the lower lid.
  • Minimum fitting height is design-dependent: short corridors need about 14 mm, standard designs 18–22 mm below the pupil centre. Choose the frame after choosing the design, not before.
  • Vertex distance as short as lashes allow, pantoscopic tilt 8–12°, and modest face form (wrap) so the lens plane follows the eye's rotation.
  • Martin rule of tilt: for every 2° of pantoscopic tilt, lower the optical centre 1 mm so the visual axis passes through the optical centre in primary gaze.
  • Non-adapt troubleshooting: verify monocular PDs and fitting heights first (the two commonest errors), then check for excessive add, unequal seg heights, wrong base curve, or an uncorrected vertical imbalance. Give a two-week adaptation window with explicit instruction to point the nose at the target.

Frame measurement and the boxing system

  • Eye size (A): horizontal box width of the lens. B: vertical box height. DBL: distance between lenses (bridge). ED: effective diameter, the longest diameter through the box centre (twice the longest radius).
  • Frame PD (geometric centre distance) = A + DBL.
  • Total decentration per lens = (frame PD − patient PD) / 2. If positive, the optical centre must move nasally.
  • Datum line: the horizontal midline of the box, used as the reference for segment heights in the datum system; the boxing system references the lowest point of the lens instead. Modern practice uses the boxing system.
  • Frames are marked A □ DBL – temple length, for example 52 □ 18 – 140.

Presbyopia and the add

Determining the near add: use at least two methods and cross-check
  1. Age-expected value as a sanity check: about +1.00 at 45, +1.50 at 50, +2.00 at 55, +2.50 at 60 and beyond.
  2. Half the remaining amplitude rule: leave half the measured amplitude of accommodation in reserve. Add = near demand − (amplitude / 2).
  3. Balance NRA and PRA: the correct add is the tentative add plus (NRA + PRA)/2. Equal NRA and PRA indicates a balanced add.
  4. Fused cross cylinder at 40 cm with a dissociating grid: the endpoint balances horizontal and vertical line clarity.
  5. Dynamic (binocular) crossed cylinder or NRA/PRA-refined trial frame check in the patient's actual working distance and posture, with the actual reading material.
  6. Verify the range of clear vision at the chosen add and confirm it brackets the working distance. Range is bounded by the add's far point (1/add) and the near point given add plus half the remaining amplitude.

Worked example. A 52-year-old bookkeeper works at 40 cm (2.50 D demand) with an amplitude of 2.00 D. Half-amplitude reserve leaves 1.00 D usable, so add = 2.50 − 1.00 = +1.50 D. At +1.50 the far point of the add is 67 cm and, using the remaining 1.00 D of reserve, the near point is 40 cm: the range is 40–67 cm, which brackets the task. If she also uses a monitor at 65 cm, that sits at the very edge of the range and an occupational progressive or a separate intermediate pair is the better answer.

Key
  1. Total +4.50; manifest +1.00; latent +3.50. Of the manifest, the part that can be overcome with normal acuity is facultative and any part leaving reduced acuity is absolute.
  2. In purely axial ametropia, correction at the eye's anterior focal point (about 15.7 mm, roughly the spectacle plane) equalises retinal image size. It can fail because axial myopia stretches the retina, reducing receptor density per degree, so the equal-sized image is sampled more coarsely.
  3. SM = [1/(1 − hFv)] × [1/(1 − (t/n)F1)]; the levers are centre thickness and front base curve (plus vertex distance).
  4. Round 22: OC is 11 mm below the top, jump = 1.1 × 2.00 = 2.20 Δ. Flat top 28: OC about 5 mm below, jump = 0.5 × 2.00 = 1.00 Δ.
  5. A round-top segment. A myope reading below the distance optical centre gets base-up prism; the round segment's base-down jump partially cancels it, minimising total displacement even though the jump itself is larger.
  6. Right: 1.0 cm × 4.00 = 4.0 Δ base-down (reading below OC of a plus lens gives base-down). Left: 1.0 × 1.00 = 1.0 Δ base-down. Imbalance 3.0 Δ, well above tolerance. Options: slab-off on the more plus lens (or the appropriate bicentric grind), dissimilar segment heights, contact lenses, or separate single-vision near glasses.
  7. Frame PD = 54 + 16 = 70 mm. Decentration = (70 − 62)/2 = 4 mm nasally per lens.
  8. The steep (Wollaston) and flat (Ostwalt) branches; the flat Ostwalt branch is used for cosmetic and weight reasons.
  9. s = y2/2r. Increasing the diameter increases y, which increases the sag for the same radius, so the lens vaults more and behaves as though fitted steeper.
  10. Activation is driven by ultraviolet light, and modern windscreens block most UV.
  11. Axis ±3° for a 1.25 D cylinder; vertical prism imbalance ±0.33 Δ.
  12. Demand at 33 cm = 3.00 D; reserve half of 2.50, so 1.25 D usable; add = 3.00 − 1.25 = +1.75 D. Far point of the add = 1/1.75 = 57 cm; near point using the 1.25 D reserve = 1/(1.75 + 1.25) = 33 cm. Range 33 to 57 cm.
Chapter recap
  1. Ametropia has five mechanisms: axial, curvature, index, positional, and retinal. Drugs cause acute myopia by ciliochoroidal effusion.
  2. Total = latent + manifest; manifest = facultative + absolute. Cycloplege every child with esotropia.
  3. 1 D of anisometropia gives 1 to 2% aniseikonia; Knapp law says correct axial error at the spectacle plane, but retinal stretch complicates it.
  4. Spectacle magnification = power factor × shape factor; thickness and base curve are what the optician can change.
  5. Jump depends on segment design and add only; total displacement adds the distance lens prism.
  6. Executive has zero jump, flat top minimal, round top maximal; pair round with myopes for least displacement.
  7. Sag formula s = y²/2r governs thickness, base curves, and contact lens vault.
  8. Polycarbonate for safety, Trivex for weight and Abbe, high index for thinness with AR.
  9. ANSI axis tolerance tightens as cylinder power rises, down to ±2° above 1.50 D; vertical imbalance limit 0.33 Δ.
  10. Determine the add by at least two methods, then verify the range of clear vision brackets the task.
