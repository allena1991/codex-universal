Embryonic origins: the table that answers a dozen items

Every ocular structure by germ layer
SourceStructures
Surface ectoderm Lens (entire), corneal epithelium, conjunctival epithelium, epidermis of the eyelids, lacrimal gland and accessory lacrimal glands, the whole lacrimal drainage epithelium, meibomian and other adnexal glands, lash follicles.
Neuroectoderm
(optic cup, from diencephalon)		 Inner layer of the optic cup → neurosensory retina, non-pigmented ciliary epithelium, posterior iris pigment epithelium.
Outer layer → retinal pigment epithelium, pigmented ciliary epithelium, anterior iris epithelium and hence the sphincter and dilator pupillae.
Also: optic nerve axons and glia (astrocytes, oligodendrocytes, Müller cells), optic nerve, part of the primary vitreous.
Neural crest
(the largest contributor)		 Corneal stroma and endothelium, Descemet membrane, Bowman layer, trabecular meshwork, sclera (most of it), choroidal stroma, iris stroma, ciliary muscle, all uveal and epithelial melanocytes, orbital bones and cartilage, orbital connective tissue and fat, meninges of the optic nerve, ciliary ganglion, Schwann cells, and the connective tissue of the lids and EOM sheaths.
Mesoderm Extraocular muscles, vascular endothelium of every ocular vessel (including the endothelium of Schlemm canal), and the temporal portion of the sclera in some accounts.
Two rules that resolve most origin items

"If it is transparent and epithelial at the front, it is surface ectoderm. If it is pigmented or neural, it is neuroectoderm. If it is connective tissue, it is neural crest. Only muscle-that-moves-the-eye and blood vessel linings are mesoderm."

The cornea is the trap: epithelium is surface ectoderm; stroma and endothelium are neural crest. Similarly the iris is split: stroma from neural crest, epithelia and both muscles from neuroectoderm.

Timeline of ocular morphogenesis

Key events with gestational timing
TimingEvent
Day 22 (wk 3)Optic sulci (pits) appear in the neural folds of the forebrain.
Day 25 (wk 4)Optic vesicles evaginate from the diencephalon, still connected by the optic stalk.
Day 27Optic vesicle induces the overlying surface ectoderm to thicken into the lens placode. This induction is the classic example of reciprocal induction: without contact, no lens forms.
Day 28–32Vesicle invaginates to form the bilayered optic cup; the lens placode invaginates as the lens pit. The inferonasal groove that admits vascular mesenchyme is the optic (embryonic, choroid) fissure.
Day 33 (wk 5)Lens vesicle separates from the surface ectoderm. Failure of separation is the mechanism of Peters anomaly and anterior lenticonus. Hyaloid artery enters through the fissure.
Wk 5–7 (day 33–44)Optic fissure closes, from the middle outward. Failure produces a typical coloboma, which is therefore inferonasal and may involve iris, ciliary body, choroid, retina, and optic disc in continuity.
Wk 5–6Primary lens fibres from the posterior lens vesicle cells form the embryonic nucleus. Extraocular muscles and lids begin.
Wk 6–7Optic nerve axons grow from ganglion cells into the optic stalk. Eyelid folds meet and begin to fuse.
Wk 8–9Eyelids fuse. Cornea has recognisable layers. Ciliary body begins. Anterior chamber appears.
Mo 3 (wk 9–12)Secondary lens fibres from the equator create the Y sutures and the fetal nucleus. Secondary (definitive, avascular) vitreous is laid down. Retinal vessels begin to grow from the disc.
Mo 4–5Ciliary processes and zonules (tertiary vitreous) form. Iris sphincter differentiates (dilator follows around month 6). Sclera condenses. Lacrimal drainage canalises from above downward.
Mo 5–7 (wk 20–26)Eyelids reopen. Photoreceptor outer segments appear. Pupillary membrane begins to regress.
Mo 7Hyaloid system regresses (from posterior to anterior). Optic nerve myelination begins at the chiasm and proceeds anteriorly.
Mo 8–9Retinal vascularisation reaches the nasal ora at about 36 weeks and the temporal ora at or shortly after term (40 weeks): the incomplete temporal periphery is exactly why retinopathy of prematurity is a temporal disease of preterm infants.
Birth to 1 moMyelination reaches the lamina cribrosa and stops. Advancing past it later gives myelinated retinal nerve fibres.
Birth to 4 moFoveal maturation: inner layers migrate away, cones elongate and pack. Not complete until about 4 months, and cone packing continues to about 4 years.
Birth to 1 yrTrabecular meshwork and angle continue to mature. Maldevelopment here produces primary congenital glaucoma (CYP1B1). Valve of Hasner canalises: failure gives congenital nasolacrimal duct obstruction, which resolves spontaneously in most infants by 12 months.

Emmetropisation is active, not passive

The retina detects defocus and modulates axial elongation through a signalling cascade involving the retina, RPE, choroid, and scleral remodelling. Hyperopic defocus (image behind the retina) accelerates axial growth; myopic defocus slows it. That single mechanism is the rationale for every modern myopia control strategy: peripheral myopic defocus with orthokeratology or multifocal soft lenses, defocus incorporated spectacle lenses, and low-dose atropine (which acts on a non-accommodative retinal or scleral pathway, since it works in chicks with no accommodative mechanism).

Risk factors for myopia progression: two myopic parents, early age of onset, less than about 1 hour of daily outdoor time, near work with sustained short working distance, and an accommodative lag with esophoria at near.

Visual acuity (behavioural) Roughly 20/400 at birth, 20/200 at 2–3 months, 20/100 at 6 months, 20/50 at 1 year, 20/30 at 2–3 years, 20/20 by 3–5 years.
Visual acuity (VEP / preferential looking) Matures faster than recognition acuity: VEP acuity approaches 20/20 by 6–12 months. Expect a discrepancy and know which test you are quoting.
Critical periods, and why they matter for amblyopia
  • Period of greatest susceptibility to deprivation: birth to about 2 years, peaking in the first 6 months. A congenital unilateral cataract must be removed within weeks, not months.
  • Period of emmetropisation and visual maturation: to about 6–8 years.
  • Period during which amblyopia can be reversed: classically to about 7–9 years, but treatment response has been demonstrated into the teens and beyond, so age alone should not withhold treatment.
  • Amblyopia severity by type: deprivation > anisometropic > strabismic > isoametropic / meridional. Deprivation is the most damaging because it removes pattern input entirely.

Human development beyond the eye

ABS items on human development also test general developmental and ageing physiology in the framework of the visual system.

Ageing changes with direct clinical consequences
SystemChange and consequence
AccommodationAmplitude falls roughly linearly (Hofstetter: mean amplitude = 18.5 − 0.30 × age); functionally presbyopic near 40–45. Mechanism: lens hardening and capsule/zonule geometry, not ciliary muscle failure.
PupilSenile miosis: resting diameter falls about 0.5 mm per decade after 20, reducing retinal illuminance and increasing depth of focus.
LensYellowing (chromophore accumulation) reduces short-wavelength transmission, producing acquired tritan colour errors; nuclear sclerosis causes myopic shift.
Tear filmReduced aqueous secretion and meibomian dropout; dry eye prevalence rises steeply with age and with female sex after menopause.
VitreousSyneresis and PVD, with peak incidence in the sixth and seventh decades, earlier in myopes and after cataract surgery.
Retina and RPELipofuscin accumulation, drusen, reduced rod density (rods are lost faster than cones with age), slower dark adaptation.
CorneaEndothelial density falls, guttae increase peripherally (Hassall-Henle), arcus senilis appears, corneal sensitivity declines.
Contrast and glareContrast sensitivity and disability glare worsen faster than Snellen acuity, which is why acuity alone under-describes functional loss in the elderly.

Key
  1. Epithelium: surface ectoderm. Stroma and endothelium: neural crest.
  2. Stroma: neural crest. Both epithelia and both the sphincter and dilator: neuroectoderm.
  3. Extraocular muscles and all vascular endothelium, including the endothelium of Schlemm canal.
  4. Because the optic (embryonic) fissure lies inferonasally and closes from the middle outward during weeks 5 to 7; failure leaves a defect along that line.
  5. Persistent fetal vasculature (PHPV); failure of regression of the primary vitreous and hyaloid system beginning at month 7.
  6. PAX6 on 11p13; Wilms tumour, via contiguous deletion of WT1 in the WAGR syndrome.
  7. Retinal vascularisation reaches the nasal ora at about 36 weeks but the temporal ora only at term, so the temporal periphery is the region left avascular in a preterm infant.
  8. Axial length 16 to 17 mm to 23 to 24 mm; corneal power about 51 D to 43 to 44 D. Corneal and lens flattening offset axial elongation, and the process is actively guided by retinal defocus.
  9. By 3 months of age.
  10. Deprivation > anisometropic > strabismic > isoametropic or meridional.
  11. Tertiary vitreous, at months 3 to 4.
  12. Optic nerve hypoplasia with possible septo-optic dysplasia (de Morsier): absent septum pellucidum and pituitary or hypothalamic dysfunction, which can be life-threatening if unrecognised.
Chapter recap
  1. Four sources: surface ectoderm for lens and surface epithelia, neuroectoderm for retina and pigmented layers and iris muscles, neural crest for all connective tissue, mesoderm for EOM and vessel linings.
  2. Optic vesicle day 25, lens placode day 27, lens vesicle day 33, fissure closed by week 7.
  3. Coloboma is inferonasal because that is where the fissure is.
  4. Primary vitreous is vascular and leaves Cloquet canal, Mittendorf dot, and Bergmeister papilla; secondary is the definitive gel; tertiary becomes zonules.
  5. Lids fuse at 8 weeks and reopen at 5 to 7 months; myelination stops at the lamina cribrosa about a month after birth.
  6. Temporal retina vascularises last, which is the whole geography of ROP.
  7. 16 to 24 mm, 51 to 43 D, 34 to 19 D: emmetropisation is a coordinated, defocus-guided process.
  8. Milestones: fix and follow by 6 to 8 weeks, accommodation by 4 months, stereopsis from 3 to 6 months, 20/20 by 3 to 5 years.
  9. Deprivation amblyopia in the first 6 months is the most damaging visual event in a human lifetime.
  10. Named anomalies map one-to-one onto failed developmental steps and to specific genes.
