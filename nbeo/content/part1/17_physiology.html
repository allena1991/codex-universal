The tear film

Tear film structure: the classical three-layer model
LayerThicknessSource and function
Lipid~0.1 µm Meibomian glands. Retards evaporation (by roughly 4–10×), lowers surface tension, and provides a smooth optical surface. Composed of wax and cholesterol esters, triglycerides, and polar lipids. Failure here causes evaporative dry eye and a rapid tear break-up time.
Aqueous~7 µm Main and accessory lacrimal glands. Carries electrolytes, glucose, oxygen, antibacterial proteins, immunoglobulins, and growth factors. Failure causes aqueous-deficient dry eye.
Mucin~0.02–0.05 µm plus a glycocalyx Goblet cells (MUC5AC) and epithelial membrane-bound mucins (MUC1, 4, 16). Converts the hydrophobic epithelial surface to a wettable one and traps debris. Failure causes surface non-wetting and staining despite normal volume.

The modern view treats the film as a two-phase structure: a lipid layer over a mucoaqueous gel whose mucin concentration rises toward the epithelium. Either model answers exam items; know the classical thicknesses.

Corneal physiology

Deturgescence and the pump-leak hypothesis

Why the cornea stays clear

Stromal glycosaminoglycans generate a swelling pressure of about 50–60 mmHg, so the stroma continuously imbibes water. Two barriers and one pump oppose it:

  1. Epithelial tight junctions: the strongest barrier to water and solute entry from tears.
  2. Endothelial barrier: leaky by design (a "pump-leak" system), allowing nutrient entry from aqueous.
  3. Endothelial Na+/K+ ATPase and bicarbonate-dependent transport, supported by carbonic anhydrase, actively pumps ions (and osmotically obligated water) from stroma into aqueous.

Net stromal hydration settles at 78% water. Endothelial dysfunction or epithelial barrier loss shifts the balance and the cornea swells; because collagen lamellae are tethered laterally, swelling occurs almost entirely in the anteroposterior direction, which is why oedema shows as increased thickness and posterior striae rather than lateral expansion.

  • Temperature reversal: cool the cornea and the pump slows while the leak continues, so the cornea swells; rewarm it and thickness recovers. This experiment established that the endothelium is an active pump.
  • Physiologic overnight oedema: closed-lid hypoxia produces 2–4% corneal thickening in a normal eye, resolving within an hour or two of waking. Contact lens wear that exceeds this is pathologic.
  • Critical oxygen requirements (Holden-Mertz): to avoid more than 4% oedema, a lens needs Dk/t ≥ 24 × 10−9 units for daily wear and Dk/t ≥ 87 for extended (overnight) wear. Harvitt and Bonanno later proposed higher targets (~35 and ~125) to avoid anoxia in any corneal layer.
  • Hypoxic sequelae in order of appearance: epithelial microcysts and vacuoles → striae (at about 5% oedema) → folds (at about 8%) → polymegathism and pleomorphism → stromal thinning → neovascularisation.
  • Corneal sensitivity is highest centrally, decreases with contact lens wear, with age, in diabetes, after LASIK, and in herpetic disease. It rises through the day and is lowest on waking.

Aqueous humour dynamics

Formation: three mechanisms

How aqueous is made
MechanismShareDetail
Active secretion80–90% Energy-dependent transport across the non-pigmented ciliary epithelium, driven by Na+/K+ ATPase with carbonic anhydrase II supplying bicarbonate. Independent of IOP. This is the step blocked by carbonic anhydrase inhibitors and by β-blockers (which reduce cAMP-driven secretion).
Ultrafiltration~10–20% Hydrostatic pressure gradient across ciliary capillaries. IOP-dependent, so it falls as IOP rises (pseudofacility).
Simple diffusionsmallLipid-soluble substances move down concentration gradients.

Flow is 2.0–3.0 µL/min (about 2.5) with a marked diurnal rhythm: production falls by roughly half during sleep. That single fact explains why nocturnal IOP does not simply track daytime IOP and why aqueous suppressants have limited nocturnal effect while prostaglandins retain it.

The Goldmann equation

Goldmann equation: know every term

Po = (F − U) / C + Pv

TermMeaningNormal value
PoIntraocular pressure~15.5 mmHg
FRate of aqueous formation2.0–3.0 µL/min
UUveoscleral (pressure-independent) outflow~0.2–0.5 µL/min, i.e. 10–20% of flow
CFacility of trabecular outflow0.2–0.3 µL/min/mmHg
PvEpiscleral venous pressure8–10 mmHg

Outflow resistance R = 1/C. Every IOP-lowering intervention acts on one of these four variables, and identifying which one is a recurring item stem:

  • Reduce F: β-blockers, α2-agonists, carbonic anhydrase inhibitors.
  • Increase C: pilocarpine, laser trabeculoplasty, trabeculotomy, MIGS stents, Rho kinase inhibitors (netarsudil also lowers Pv).
  • Increase U: prostaglandin analogues, α2-agonists (partly), cyclodialysis.
  • Reduce Pv: netarsudil; conversely a raised Pv from carotid-cavernous fistula, Sturge-Weber, thyroid eye disease, or superior vena cava obstruction raises IOP and shows blood in Schlemm canal on gonioscopy.

Intraocular pressure and tonometry

  • Population mean 15.5 mmHg, standard deviation 2.6 mmHg. The distribution is not Gaussian: it is skewed to the right, which is why 21 mmHg (mean + 2 SD) is a statistical convention rather than a physiologic threshold.
  • Diurnal variation 3–5 mmHg in normal eyes and often more in glaucoma; typically highest in the early morning. Supine posture raises IOP by 2–6 mmHg.
  • Factors that raise IOP: supine position, Valsalva, breath-holding, tight collar, eyelid squeezing, water drinking, caffeine (small), corticosteroids, and inverted body positions. Factors that lower it: aerobic exercise, general anaesthesia, alcohol, metabolic acidosis, and pregnancy.
  • IOP rises modestly with age in most populations, and is higher with higher blood pressure and body mass index.
Imbert-Fick and the four assumptions Goldmann tonometry makes

Imbert-Fick principle: P = F / A, valid only for a sphere that is (1) infinitely thin, (2) perfectly elastic, (3) perfectly spherical, and (4) dry. The cornea satisfies none of these, so Goldmann engineered a compromise:

  • The applanated diameter is fixed at 3.06 mm (area 7.35 mm²), the diameter at which the force resisting applanation (corneal rigidity) is exactly balanced by the force assisting it (tear film surface tension, capillary attraction).
  • At that diameter, grams of force × 10 = mmHg, which is why the dial is calibrated the way it is.
  • Calibration assumes a central corneal thickness of about 520 µm.

Consequences. A thicker cornea reads falsely high; a thinner cornea reads falsely low (roughly 0.5–0.7 mmHg per 10 µm, though the relationship is not linear and correction nomograms are approximations). Thin corneas are also an independent risk factor for glaucoma progression in the Ocular Hypertension Treatment Study, so do not simply "correct away" a low reading. Corneal astigmatism above 3 D requires rotating the prism so the red mark aligns with the minus cylinder axis, or averaging two readings 90° apart. Excess fluorescein gives wide mires and overestimates; too little gives thin mires and underestimates.

Accommodation

Helmholtz mechanism, and the six changes of the near triad

Helmholtz (capsular) theory: parasympathetic stimulation contracts the ciliary muscle, which moves the ciliary body forward and inward, relaxing zonular tension; the elastic lens capsule then moulds the lens into a more convex shape. Accommodation is therefore an active process producing a passive lens deformation.

What changes on accommodating:

  1. Anterior lens surface steepens markedly (radius 10 → ~6 mm); posterior surface steepens slightly.
  2. Lens axial thickness increases; equatorial diameter decreases.
  3. Anterior lens pole moves forward, so anterior chamber depth decreases.
  4. Lens nucleus density redistributes; the gradient index steepens.
  5. Pupil constricts (part of the near triad, increasing depth of focus).
  6. Convergence occurs (the third element of the near triad).

Presbyopia is primarily lenticular: the lens and capsule stiffen and the lens grows, so the same ciliary effort produces less shape change. The ciliary muscle itself remains functional, which is why accommodating intraocular lenses remain an active field.

  • Hofstetter formulae for amplitude of accommodation in dioptres: maximum = 25 − 0.4 × age; mean = 18.5 − 0.30 × age; minimum = 15 − 0.25 × age.
  • Types of accommodation: reflex (blur-driven, up to about 2 D), vergence (driven by disparity, via the AC/A relationship), proximal (awareness of nearness), and tonic (the resting state, about 1 D in the dark: "dark focus").
  • Accommodative lag of about +0.25 to +0.75 D is normal on monocular estimate method retinoscopy; a high lag with esophoria at near is the classic accommodative-insufficiency pattern and a putative myopia risk factor.

Retinal physiology and electrodiagnostics

Adaptation

  • Dark adaptation curve is biphasic: a rapid cone limb plateauing at 5–7 minutes, then the rod-cone break, then a slow rod limb reaching maximum sensitivity at 20–40 minutes. Total sensitivity gain is about 5 log units.
  • Purkinje shift: peak spectral sensitivity moves from 555 nm (photopic, cones) to 507 nm (scotopic, rods), which is why red objects look darker and blue objects relatively brighter at dusk.
  • Photopic above about 3 cd/m², mesopic between, scotopic below about 0.003 cd/m².
  • Stiles-Crawford effect: cones are most sensitive to light entering through the pupil centre; this waveguide directionality reduces the visual impact of scattered and peripheral rays.

Receptive fields and lateral inhibition

  • Ganglion cells have concentric centre-surround antagonistic receptive fields: ON-centre cells depolarise to light in the centre and are inhibited by surround light; OFF-centre cells do the reverse. The surround is created by horizontal cell lateral inhibition in the outer plexiform layer and by amacrine cells in the inner.
  • Consequences: the retina encodes contrast, not absolute luminance; simultaneous contrast, Mach bands, and the Hermann grid illusion are direct behavioural read-outs of lateral inhibition.
  • Receptive field size increases with eccentricity, which is the physiologic basis of the acuity gradient across the field.
  • Cortex: V1 simple cells respond to oriented edges at a specific retinal position; complex cells are position-invariant; hypercomplex cells are end-stopped. Cells are organised into orientation columns, ocular dominance columns (the anatomic substrate modified in amblyopia), and cytochrome oxidase blobs for colour. Beyond V1, the dorsal stream (magno, to parietal) handles motion and spatial relations ("where") and the ventral stream (parvo, to temporal) handles form and colour ("what").

Electrodiagnostic tests: what each one localises

ERG, EOG, VEP: components and origins
TestComponentCellular origin and clinical use
Full-field ERGa-wave (negative) Photoreceptors (outer segment hyperpolarisation). Reduced in photoreceptor disease.
b-wave (positive) ON bipolar cells, recorded via Müller cell potassium currents. A selectively reduced b-wave with a normal a-wave (negative ERG) localises to the inner retina: congenital stationary night blindness, X-linked retinoschisis, CRAO, quinine toxicity, melanoma-associated retinopathy.

EOGArden ratio = light peak / dark trough Requires a healthy RPE and photoreceptor interface. Normal >1.8; borderline 1.65–1.8; subnormal <1.65. A markedly abnormal EOG with a normal ERG is the signature of Best vitelliform dystrophy (bestrophin, BEST1).

Delayed latency with relatively preserved amplitude means demyelination (optic neuritis, MS); reduced amplitude with normal latency means axonal loss (compression, ischaemia, glaucoma). Sweep VEP estimates infant acuity.
Three electrodiagnostic patterns worth memorising as pairs
  • Normal ERG, abnormal EOG → Best disease.
  • Reduced a- and b-wave → widespread photoreceptor disease (retinitis pigmentosa: rod responses affected first and most).
  • Normal a-wave, reduced b-wave → inner retinal or bipolar dysfunction.
  • Normal ERG, abnormal VEP → optic nerve or cortex, not retina.
  • Normal ERG and VEP with poor vision → consider non-organic visual loss.

Extraocular muscle and binocular physiology

The two innervational laws and their clinical use
LawStatementClinical application
Sherrington law of reciprocal innervation Contraction of a muscle is accompanied by relaxation of its ipsilateral antagonist. Explains why forced duction testing distinguishes restriction (mechanical) from paresis, and why the antagonist does not resist a normal saccade.
Hering law of equal innervation Yoke muscles in the two eyes receive equal and simultaneous innervation. Explains secondary deviation exceeding primary deviation in a paretic strabismus, and enhancement of ptosis in the fellow lid when the ptotic eye is made to fixate in myasthenia. Also underlies the three-step test logic.
  • Blood-ocular barriers: blood-aqueous (non-pigmented ciliary epithelium tight junctions plus non-fenestrated iris capillaries) and blood-retinal (retinal capillary endothelium inner, RPE outer). Their breakdown produces, respectively, cells and flare, and macular oedema.
  • Autoregulation: retinal and optic nerve head vessels autoregulate over a wide perfusion pressure range and have no autonomic innervation; the choroid does not autoregulate and is richly innervated. Ocular perfusion pressure = mean arterial pressure − IOP, which is why nocturnal systemic hypotension is a glaucoma risk factor.
  • Aqueous flow direction: posterior chamber → pupil → anterior chamber → angle. Obstruction at the pupil gives iris bombé; obstruction behind the iris or lens gives malignant (aqueous misdirection) glaucoma, which does not respond to iridotomy.

Key
  1. Po = (F − U)/C + Pv. β-blockers, α2-agonists, and CAIs reduce F; pilocarpine, SLT, and MIGS raise C; prostaglandins raise U; netarsudil raises C and lowers Pv.
  2. Pv, episcleral venous pressure; carotid-cavernous fistula and Sturge-Weber (also thyroid eye disease, SVC obstruction).
  3. Cooling the cornea causes swelling because the active pump slows while passive leak continues; rewarming reverses it. It proved the endothelium is an active, energy-dependent pump.
  4. Dk/t of at least 24 for daily wear and 87 for extended wear, designed to keep oedema under about 4%.
  5. At that diameter the force of corneal rigidity resisting applanation is balanced by the tear surface tension assisting it, so the reading reflects IOP alone.
  6. Either the true IOP is lower than measured because of the thick cornea, or the thick cornea is a real biomechanical feature and the eye genuinely has elevated pressure. Thickness correction is approximate, so treat trends, discs, and fields rather than a corrected number alone.
  7. Dynamic contour tonometry (Pascal).
  8. Anterior surface steepens, posterior steepens slightly, lens thickens axially and narrows equatorially, anterior pole moves forward reducing chamber depth, pupil constricts, and the eyes converge.
  9. 18.5 − 0.30 × age; at 45 that is 18.5 − 13.5 = 5.0 D.
  10. ON bipolar cells, recorded through Müller cell potassium currents; a normal a-wave with a reduced b-wave (negative ERG) localises to the inner retina, as in congenital stationary night blindness, X-linked retinoschisis, CRAO, or quinine toxicity.
  11. Best vitelliform macular dystrophy; BEST1 encoding bestrophin.
  12. Delayed latency with preserved amplitude indicates demyelination; reduced amplitude with normal latency indicates axonal loss.
  13. Yoke muscles receive equal innervation. When the more ptotic eye fixates, extra innervation goes to both levators; occluding it removes that drive and the fellow lid falls.
  14. Retinal and optic nerve head vessels.
Chapter recap
  1. Three tear layers, 7 µm total, 16% turnover per minute, TBUT above 10 seconds.
  2. Deturgescence = epithelial barrier plus a leaky endothelium plus an active pump against a 50 to 60 mmHg swelling pressure.
  3. Dk/t 24 daily, 87 extended; hypoxia gives microcysts, then striae at 5%, then folds at 8%.
  4. Aqueous: 80 to 90% active secretion, 2.5 µL/min, halved at night.
  5. Goldmann equation with five terms, and every drug maps to one of them.
  6. IOP 15.5 ± 2.6 with a right-skewed distribution; 21 is a convention.
  7. Imbert-Fick needs a thin, elastic, spherical, dry sphere; 3.06 mm is the engineered compromise.
  8. Helmholtz: contraction slackens zonules and the capsule moulds the lens; presbyopia is lenticular.
  9. ERG a-wave photoreceptors, b-wave ON bipolars via Müller, c-wave RPE; EOG tests RPE; VEP latency means myelin, amplitude means axons.
  10. Sherrington for antagonists, Hering for yokes; retinal vessels autoregulate, choroidal vessels do not.
