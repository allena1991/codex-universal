Hypersensitivity types

"ACID": type I Allergic (anaphylactic), type II Cytotoxic, type III Immune complex, type IV Delayed.

Ocular immune privilege

Why a corneal graft succeeds without HLA matching
  1. No lymphatics and no blood vessels in the cornea, lens, or vitreous. Antigens cannot reach a draining node by the usual route.
  2. Blood-ocular barriers restrict lymphocyte and antibody entry.
  3. An immunosuppressive microenvironment: aqueous contains TGF-β2, α-melanocyte stimulating hormone, VIP, calcitonin gene-related peptide, somatostatin, and complement regulatory proteins.
  4. Fas ligand expression on corneal endothelium and other ocular tissues induces apoptosis of infiltrating activated T cells.
  5. Low MHC class II expression and few resident antigen-presenting cells in the central cornea (Langerhans cells are limbal).
  6. Anterior chamber associated immune deviation (ACAID): antigen introduced into the anterior chamber is carried by F4/80-positive antigen-presenting cells through the trabecular meshwork into the blood, reaching the spleen, where it induces regulatory T cells that suppress the delayed hypersensitivity response to that antigen systemically.

The price of privilege: once these mechanisms are breached, as when a graft becomes vascularised or a herpetic cornea becomes inflamed, rejection risk rises sharply. And privilege explains sympathetic ophthalmia: penetrating trauma exposing uveal antigen through a lymphatic-bearing wound bypasses privilege and sensitises the host against its own uvea, producing bilateral granulomatous panuveitis with Dalen-Fuchs nodules weeks to years later.

The organisms that penetrate an intact corneal epithelium

"Shigella, Corynebacterium diphtheriae, Neisseria gonorrhoeae, Neisseria meningitidis, Haemophilus (aegyptius / influenzae biotype III), Listeria"

The high-yield trio to remember is Neisseria gonorrhoeae, Corynebacterium diphtheriae, and Shigella. Pseudomonas is also capable of rapid invasion, especially in the presence of a contact lens biofilm. All other bacteria require an epithelial break.

Viruses

Ocular viral pathogens
VirusDisease patterns and key facts
Adenovirus (double-stranded DNA, non-enveloped) Five patterns: (1) non-specific follicular conjunctivitis; (2) pharyngoconjunctival fever (serotypes 3, 4, 7) with fever and pharyngitis in children; (3) epidemic keratoconjunctivitis (serotypes 8, 19, 37) with subepithelial infiltrates at 7–14 days, membranes, and preauricular adenopathy; (4) chronic; (5) acute haemorrhagic. Extremely contagious for 10 to 14 days; non-enveloped, so it survives on surfaces and resists alcohol. Treat supportively; steroids relieve but prolong shedding.
Herpes simplex virus 1 and 2 Primary: blepharoconjunctivitis with vesicles and follicles. Recurrent: dendritic epithelial keratitis with terminal bulbs (staining centrally with fluorescein and at the edges with rose bengal or lissamine), geographic ulcer, stromal (immune) keratitis with a Wessely ring, disciform endotheliitis, neurotrophic keratopathy, iritis with sectoral iris atrophy and raised IOP, and acute retinal necrosis. Latency in the trigeminal ganglion. HEDS trials: topical steroids help stromal keratitis (with antiviral cover), oral aciclovir 400 mg twice daily halves recurrence.
Varicella zoster virus Chickenpox, then latency in the trigeminal ganglion. Herpes zoster ophthalmicus: dermatomal V1 vesicles, pseudodendrites (elevated, mucous, no terminal bulbs), stromal keratitis, uveitis with sectoral atrophy, scleritis, cranial nerve palsies, post-herpetic neuralgia. Hutchinson sign predicts ocular involvement. Acute retinal necrosis in the immunocompetent; progressive outer retinal necrosis in AIDS. Ramsay Hunt when the geniculate ganglion is involved.
Cytomegalovirus Retinitis with a "pizza pie" or "brushfire" appearance: confluent necrosis, haemorrhage, and mild vitritis, classically when CD4 is below 50 cells/µL. Also causes corneal endotheliitis with linear keratic precipitates and hypertensive anterior uveitis in immunocompetent adults, and congenital infection with chorioretinitis and hearing loss.
Epstein-Barr virusDacryoadenitis, follicular conjunctivitis, nummular keratitis, and an association with ocular adnexal lymphoma.
Molluscum contagiosum (poxvirus)Umbilicated lid margin nodule causing a chronic follicular conjunctivitis by viral shedding; multiple lesions suggest immunosuppression.
Human papillomavirusConjunctival and lid papilloma (types 6, 11); ocular surface squamous neoplasia association (types 16, 18).
Rubella (congenital)Nuclear cataract, salt-and-pepper retinopathy, microphthalmia, glaucoma, plus deafness and cardiac defects.
Rubeola (measles)Keratitis, and it precipitates blindness in vitamin A deficient children.
HIVImmunosuppression enabling CMV retinitis, toxoplasmosis, PORN, cryptococcal choroiditis; direct effects include HIV retinopathy with cotton wool spots and conjunctival microvasculopathy.

Fungi, parasites, and prions

Non-bacterial, non-viral ocular pathogens
OrganismPresentation and diagnostic clue
Filamentous fungi: Fusarium, Aspergillus Keratitis after vegetative trauma or contaminated contact lens solution. Grey-white infiltrate with feathery margins, satellite lesions, an endothelial plaque, and a hypopyon with a relatively intact epithelium. Grows on Sabouraud agar; visible with calcofluor white, Giemsa, or Gomori methenamine silver. Treat with topical natamycin 5% (best evidence for Fusarium) or voriconazole. Do not give steroids.
Yeast: Candida albicans Keratitis in a compromised surface (chronic steroid use, dry eye, penetrating keratoplasty), and endogenous endophthalmitis with a fluffy white "string of pearls" in the vitreous in intravenous drug users and patients with indwelling lines.
Acanthamoeba Contact lens wearer, tap water or hot tub exposure. Pain grossly out of proportion to signs, radial perineuritis early, then a ring infiltrate; frequently misdiagnosed as HSV. Cysts are double-walled; culture on non-nutrient agar with an E. coli overlay; confocal microscopy is useful. Treat with biguanides (PHMB or chlorhexidine) plus a diamidine, for months.
Toxoplasma gondii The commonest cause of infectious posterior uveitis worldwide. Focal necrotising retinochoroiditis adjacent to a pigmented scar ("headlight in the fog" with vitritis). Cat definitive host; undercooked meat and oocysts are the routes. Congenital form is bilateral and macular. Treat significant lesions with pyrimethamine, sulfadiazine, and folinic acid, or trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, plus steroid cover.
Toxocara canis Unilateral, in a child with pica and puppy exposure. Peripheral or posterior pole granuloma with a traction band to the disc, or a diffuse endophthalmitis. No systemic eosinophilia in ocular larva migrans, unlike visceral disease. A major leukocoria differential.
Onchocerca volvulus River blindness, blackfly (Simulium) vector. Sclerosing keratitis, iritis, chorioretinal atrophy, optic atrophy. Treated with ivermectin; do not use diethylcarbamazine, which causes a severe inflammatory reaction.
Loa loa, cysticercosis, Taenia soliumSubconjunctival migrating worm; intraocular cysticercus cyst.
Demodex folliculorum and brevisMite in lash follicles and meibomian glands; cylindrical dandruff (collarettes) at the lash base. Treated with tea tree oil derivatives or lotilaner.
Phthirus pubisLash infestation with nits; a marker of sexual abuse in children.
PrionsCreutzfeldt-Jakob disease: Heidenhain variant with cortical visual loss; a theoretical transmission risk in corneal transplantation.

When to culture a corneal ulcer

Culture before treating if the infiltrate is central, larger than about 1–2 mm, involves the deep stroma, has an anterior chamber reaction, is unresponsive to initial therapy, or occurs in an atypical host (post-surgical, contact lens associated, immunocompromised). Scrape the edge and base of the ulcer, not the centre of the slough, and plate directly. Small peripheral non-vision-threatening infiltrates can be treated empirically.

Key
  1. Type I; type II; type III; type IV.
  2. No lymphatics or vessels in cornea/lens/vitreous, blood-ocular barriers, immunosuppressive aqueous factors (TGF-β2 and neuropeptides), Fas ligand expression, low MHC class II with few central antigen-presenting cells, and ACAID.
  3. Anterior chamber associated immune deviation: antigen from the anterior chamber induces systemic regulatory T cells that suppress delayed hypersensitivity; the spleen is essential.
  4. A29 birdshot chorioretinopathy; B51 Behçet disease; B27 acute anterior uveitis with the spondyloarthropathies.
  5. Neisseria gonorrhoeae, Neisseria meningitidis, Corynebacterium diphtheriae, Shigella, Haemophilus (aegyptius / influenzae biotype III), and Listeria; Pseudomonas can invade rapidly in the presence of a lens biofilm.
  6. HSV gives a true dendrite with terminal bulbs and an ulcerated base; VZV gives an elevated mucous pseudodendrite without terminal bulbs.
  7. Serotypes 8, 19, and 37; subepithelial infiltrates appear at about 7 to 14 days.
  8. Acanthamoeba; non-nutrient agar with an E. coli overlay; a biguanide (PHMB or chlorhexidine) plus a diamidine.
  9. Cutibacterium (Propionibacterium) acnes.
  10. Below about 50 cells/µL; confluent necrotising retinitis with haemorrhage and mild vitritis, described as pizza pie or brushfire.
  11. Ocular toxocariasis; systemic eosinophilia is characteristically absent in the ocular form.
  12. Lysozyme and lactoferrin; secretory IgA.
Chapter recap
  1. ACID: allergic, cytotoxic, immune complex, delayed. Assign every inflammatory eye disease to one.
  2. Immune privilege has six pillars, and breaking them explains both graft rejection and sympathetic ophthalmia.
  3. HLA: B27 anterior uveitis, B51 Behçet, A29 birdshot, DR4 VKH.
  4. Only a short list of bacteria cross an intact epithelium; everything else needs a break.
  5. Contact lens plus water equals Acanthamoeba; contact lens plus overnight wear equals Pseudomonas; vegetation equals fungus.
  6. Adenovirus 8, 19, 37 for EKC; infiltrates at 7 to 14 days; contagious for two weeks.
  7. HSV dendrite has terminal bulbs; VZV pseudodendrite does not.
  8. CMV retinitis below CD4 of 50; toxoplasmosis sits next to an old scar.
  9. C. acnes for chronic postoperative inflammation with a capsular plaque; S. epidermidis for acute.
  10. Culture central, large, deep, or atypical ulcers; scrape the edge, not the slough.
