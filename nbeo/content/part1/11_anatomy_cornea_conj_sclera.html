Keratometry index trap

The keratometer measures only the anterior surface but reports total corneal power by using a fictitious index of 1.3375 instead of the true 1.376. After myopic refractive surgery this assumption fails, because the surgery changes the anterior surface without changing the posterior, so keratometry overestimates corneal power and IOL calculations go wrong. That is the anatomic reason post-LASIK IOL formulas exist.

Epithelium

  • 5–7 cell layers, 50–52 µm, about 10% of total thickness. Turnover 7–10 days.
  • Basal columnar cells: the only layer capable of mitosis. Attached to the basal lamina by hemidesmosomes anchored by anchoring fibrils (type VII collagen) into the stroma. Disruption of this adhesion complex is the mechanism of recurrent corneal erosion and of epithelial basement membrane dystrophy.
  • Wing cells: transitional, polyhedral, joined by desmosomes and gap junctions.
  • Superficial squamous cells: bear microvilli and microplicae that anchor the mucin layer, and are joined by zonulae occludentes (tight junctions). Those tight junctions are the ocular surface's principal barrier to fluorescein, drugs, and pathogens: break them and the cornea stains.
  • Epithelium is ~10× more permeable to lipophilic than hydrophilic molecules, while the stroma is the reverse. A drug must therefore be biphasic to penetrate, which is why prodrug esters (dipivefrin, latanoprost) exist.

Guttae versus Hassall-Henle bodies

Identical histology, different location. Peripheral excrescences of Descemet membrane are Hassall-Henle bodies and are a normal age change. Central excrescences are guttae and define Fuchs endothelial corneal dystrophy.

Stroma and the physics of transparency

The stroma is ~90% of corneal thickness, built from 200–250 lamellae of type I collagen running parallel to the surface and crossing at angles between lamellae. Keratocytes occupy the interlamellar spaces and are joined by gap junctions.

Why the cornea is transparent
  1. Fibril diameter is small and uniform (22–35 nm), well below the wavelength of visible light.
  2. Fibril spacing is regular and short (centre to centre ~60 nm), so scattered wavefronts from adjacent fibrils interfere destructively: the Maurice lattice theory.
  3. Avascularity.
  4. Relative dehydration to 78% water, actively maintained. Above roughly 80% hydration the lattice spacing becomes irregular and the cornea scatters: clinical oedema.
  5. Smooth optical surfaces maintained by the tear film and the epithelium.

The sclera has the same collagen types but irregular fibril diameters (25–230 nm) and irregular spacing, plus higher hydration. That irregularity is the entire reason it is white and the cornea is clear.

Glycosaminoglycans: keratan sulfate is the predominant corneal GAG, with chondroitin and dermatan sulfate also present. GAGs are strongly hydrophilic and generate the stromal swelling pressure that the endothelium must continuously oppose. Keratan sulfate accumulates in macular corneal dystrophy, which is why that dystrophy stains with Alcian blue and colloidal iron and is the only classic stromal dystrophy that is autosomal recessive.

Human endothelium is effectively amitotic: healing occurs by cell enlargement (polymegathism, rising coefficient of variation) and shape change (pleomorphism, falling percentage of hexagonal cells) rather than division. Both indices worsen before density does, so they are the early markers of contact-lens-induced hypoxic stress.

Corneal innervation

  • 70–80 stromal nerve trunks derived from the long ciliary nerves (nasociliary branch of V1) enter radially at the mid-peripheral mid-stroma.
  • They lose their myelin sheaths 1–2 mm inside the limbus, which preserves transparency, then form the subepithelial and subbasal nerve plexus before terminating as free nerve endings between epithelial cells.
  • The cornea is the most densely innervated tissue in the body, roughly 300–600× the density of skin. Sensitivity is highest centrally.
  • Nerves are trophic as well as sensory. Denervation (herpes zoster, diabetes, post-LASIK, CN V lesions) produces neurotrophic keratopathy with poor healing despite an adequate tear film.
Nutrition of an avascular tissue

Oxygen comes almost entirely from the atmosphere dissolved in the tear film (from the palpebral conjunctival vessels during sleep), which is why contact lens oxygen transmissibility is a corneal, not a conjunctival, problem. Glucose comes from the aqueous humour posteriorly. The peripheral cornea additionally receives from the limbal arcades. Waste and lactate leave via aqueous and tears.

The limbus

A 1–2 mm transition zone in which corneal structures convert to conjunctival and scleral ones. It contains, in a single small volume, most of the anterior segment's clinically critical anatomy.

  • Palisades of Vogt: radial ridges of pigmented limbal epithelium housing the limbal epithelial stem cells. Densest superiorly and inferiorly. Their loss (chemical burn, Stevens-Johnson, aniridia, contact lens overwear) produces limbal stem cell deficiency with conjunctivalisation and persistent epithelial defects.
  • Bowman layer terminates; the epithelium thickens to 10–12 layers.
  • Descemet membrane terminates at Schwalbe line.
  • The internal scleral sulcus houses the trabecular meshwork and Schlemm canal; the scleral spur is the posterior lip.
  • Limbal arcades and the terminal anterior ciliary vessels arrive here, along with the marginal corneal arcade: the substrate for limbal flush in iritis and for peripheral corneal infiltrates.
  • Thoft X, Y, Z hypothesis of epithelial maintenance: X = proliferation of basal cells, Y = centripetal migration from the limbus, Z = epithelial cell loss from the surface. X + Y must equal Z for a stable epithelium.

Histology

  • Epithelium: 2–5 layers of non-keratinised stratified squamous over the tarsus and bulbar surface, becoming more columnar in the fornices, with interspersed goblet cells (about 1.5 million total). Also contains melanocytes, Langerhans cells, and intraepithelial lymphocytes.
  • Substantia propria (stroma), two layers:
    • Adenoid (lymphoid) layer, superficial, containing lymphocytes, plasma cells, mast cells, and the conjunctiva-associated lymphoid tissue (CALT) with follicles. It does not develop until roughly 2 to 3 months of age.
    • Fibrous layer, deeper, carrying vessels and nerves, and fusing with the tarsus.
Two age-driven diagnostic rules from one histologic fact

Because the adenoid layer is absent at birth, a neonate cannot mount a follicular response. Neonatal chlamydial conjunctivitis therefore presents as a papillary, not follicular, conjunctivitis, whereas in an adult the same organism gives florid follicles. Conversely, membrane formation is prominent in the neonate.

Papilla = a vascular core with fibrous septa tethering the epithelium to the tarsus, so the vessel is central. Seen in allergic and bacterial disease.
Follicle = a lymphoid germinal centre, avascular in the middle with vessels encircling the base. Seen in viral, chlamydial, and toxic disease.

Vessels, lymphatics, and nerves

  • Arteries: the palpebral arcades supply the palpebral and forniceal conjunctiva and the posterior conjunctival arteries continue onto the peripheral bulbar conjunctiva; the anterior ciliary arteries supply the perilimbal conjunctiva and give the anterior episcleral plexus.
  • Distinguishing the plexuses at the slit lamp:
    • Superficial conjunctival vessels: bright red, move with the conjunctiva on cotton-bud manipulation, blanch with topical phenylephrine 2.5%. Injection here is conjunctival.
    • Superficial episcleral plexus: salmon pink, radial, moves less, blanches partially. Injected in episcleritis.
    • Deep episcleral plexus: violaceous, does not move and does not blanch with phenylephrine. Injected in scleritis and in anterior uveitis (ciliary flush).
  • Lymphatics: the conjunctiva and lids are the only ocular structures with lymphatic drainage (the globe itself has none, which underpins ocular immune privilege). Lateral two thirds drain to the preauricular (parotid) nodes; medial one third drains to the submandibular nodes. Preauricular lymphadenopathy is therefore a cardinal sign of adenoviral and chlamydial conjunctivitis.
  • Sensory innervation: V1 via the lacrimal, infratrochlear, supratrochlear, and long ciliary nerves; the inferior conjunctiva additionally by the infraorbital nerve (V2).

  • Scleral radius of curvature ~11.5 mm; hydration 65–70%; it becomes translucent when dehydrated, which is why a drying sclera looks bluish at surgery.
  • Layers: episclera (vascular, loose, fused with Tenon capsule), scleral stroma (dense irregular type I and III collagen with fibroblasts, sparse vessels), and the lamina fusca (inner, pigmented, with melanocytes, blending into the suprachoroidal space).
  • Emissaria (channels through the sclera):
    • Anterior (near the limbus): anterior ciliary arteries and veins, aqueous veins.
    • Middle (at the equator): the four to eight vortex veins.
    • Posterior: long and short posterior ciliary arteries, long and short ciliary nerves, and the optic nerve through the lamina cribrosa.
  • The sclera is largely avascular and nourished by the episcleral and choroidal circulations; it is richly supplied by sensory ciliary nerves, which is why scleritis produces boring, night-waking pain and episcleritis does not.
  • Blue sclera means thin sclera revealing underlying uvea: osteogenesis imperfecta, Ehlers-Danlos, Marfan, and normal infancy.

The stromal arithmetic you must be able to do

Residual stromal bed after LASIK should be at least 250–300 µm, and total residual corneal thickness at least 400 µm. Roughly 12–15 µm of central stroma is ablated per dioptre of myopia in a 6 mm optical zone (Munnerlyn: ablation depth ≈ diopters × (optical zone diameter)2 / 3).

Worked example. CCT 530 µm, flap 110 µm, correction −6.00 D at a 6 mm zone. Ablation ≈ 6 × 36 / 3 = 72 µm. Residual bed = 530 − 110 − 72 = 348 µm: acceptable. Repeat with CCT 480 µm and the bed is 298 µm: marginal, and PRK or SMILE becomes the better anatomic choice.

Key
  1. Basal columnar layer; hemidesmosomes to the basal lamina with type VII collagen anchoring fibrils into the stroma.
  2. Fibril diameter is uniform and smaller than the wavelength of light, and fibril spacing is regular so scattered light interferes destructively (Maurice lattice).
  3. Birth forceps injury and acute hydrops in keratoconus (also congenital glaucoma with Haab striae).
  4. Central = guttae, pathologic, Fuchs dystrophy. Peripheral = Hassall-Henle bodies, a normal age change.
  5. Keratan sulfate; macular corneal dystrophy (autosomal recessive).
  6. The epithelium favours lipophilic molecules and the stroma favours hydrophilic ones, so a drug must have both characters to cross both.
  7. The adenoid (lymphoid) layer of the substantia propria does not develop until about 2 to 3 months of age, so no germinal centres can form.
  8. Deep episcleral plexus; scleritis and anterior uveitis (ciliary flush).
  9. Preauricular (parotid) nodes; adenoviral and chlamydial conjunctivitis.
  10. Sclera immediately behind the rectus insertions at about 0.3 mm; inadvertent scleral perforation during strabismus surgery, and staphyloma formation in scleritis or high myopia.
  11. Ablation ≈ 5 × 42.25 / 3 ≈ 70 µm. Residual bed = 505 − 100 − 70 = 335 µm: acceptable, but total residual thickness and topography must also be checked.
  12. Palisades of Vogt; chemical burn, Stevens-Johnson syndrome, aniridia, or contact lens overwear.
Chapter recap
  1. Cornea: 12 mm wide, 540 µm central, 7.8 front / 6.8 back radius, index 1.376, +48.8 and −5.8 giving +43 D, two thirds of ocular power.
  2. Six layers, and only three of them regenerate: epithelium, Descemet, and (as scar) stroma. Bowman never does; endothelium never divides.
  3. Transparency = uniform small fibrils, regular spacing, avascularity, 78% hydration, smooth surface. The sclera fails all but the first.
  4. Endothelium 2000–3000/mm², losing 0.6% per year, decompensating near 500. Polymegathism and pleomorphism precede density loss.
  5. Long ciliary nerves from V1 make the cornea the most innervated tissue in the body and make denervation a healing problem.
  6. Limbus = palisades of Vogt, Schwalbe line, trabecular meshwork, Schlemm canal, scleral spur, limbal arcades, in 1 to 2 mm.
  7. Conjunctival adenoid layer arrives at 2 to 3 months: no follicles in a neonate. Papilla is central-vessel, follicle is peripheral-vessel.
  8. Phenylephrine blanches conjunctival and superficial episcleral vessels, not the deep episcleral plexus.
  9. Lateral conjunctiva to preauricular nodes, medial to submandibular. The globe has no lymphatics.
  10. Sclera: 1.0 mm posteriorly, 0.3 mm behind rectus insertions; three sets of emissaria; pain because of ciliary nerves.
