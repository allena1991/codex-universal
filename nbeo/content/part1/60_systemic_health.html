Diabetes mellitus

Diagnosis and control targets
TestNormalPrediabetesDiabetes
Fasting plasma glucose<100 mg/dL100–125≥126
2-hour oral glucose tolerance<140 mg/dL140–199≥200
HgbA1c<5.7%5.7–6.4%≥6.5%
Random glucose with symptoms––≥200
  • HgbA1c reflects mean glycaemia over the preceding 2–3 months (erythrocyte lifespan). General target <7%, individualised. Roughly, an A1c of 6% corresponds to a mean glucose of 126 mg/dL, and each 1% rise adds about 29 mg/dL. It is falsely low in haemolysis, recent transfusion, and pregnancy, and falsely high in iron deficiency and uraemia. Part II PAM explicitly expects you to interpret an A1c without being given a reference range, and the same expectation applies on Part I.
  • DCCT (type 1) and UKPDS (type 2): intensive glycaemic control substantially reduces the incidence and progression of retinopathy, nephropathy, and neuropathy. UKPDS additionally showed that tight blood pressure control independently reduces retinopathy progression and visual loss. Note the early worsening phenomenon: a rapid improvement in control can transiently worsen retinopathy over 6 to 12 months, so patients with significant retinopathy who suddenly gain control need closer follow-up.

Diabetes beyond the retina
  • Refractive shifts with glycaemic swings (index myopia with hyperglycaemia; hyperopic shift as control improves): do not prescribe glasses during a period of unstable control.
  • Reduced accommodation and earlier presbyopia.
  • Cranial neuropathies: pupil-sparing third nerve palsy, sixth and fourth nerve palsies, usually resolving in 8 to 12 weeks.
  • Corneal: reduced sensation and neurotrophic keratopathy, recurrent erosion from abnormal basement membrane adhesion, delayed healing, increased endothelial polymegathism.
  • Iris: poor dilation, pigment epithelial vacuolation (lacy vacuolisation), rubeosis.
  • Cataract: earlier nuclear and cortical cataract, plus true snowflake cataract in young type 1 patients.
  • Glaucoma: increased risk of primary open-angle glaucoma, and neovascular glaucoma from proliferative disease.
  • Infection: increased risk of orbital cellulitis, and mucormycosis in ketoacidosis: a black necrotic eschar with orbital apex signs is a surgical emergency.
  • NAION and papillopathy (diabetic papillopathy: bilateral disc swelling with surprisingly good acuity).
  • Screening rules: type 1 at 5 years after diagnosis or at puberty; type 2 at diagnosis; pregnancy before conception or in the first trimester and then each trimester, since pregnancy accelerates retinopathy (gestational diabetes alone does not require retinal screening).

Vascular ocular emergencies and their systemic meaning
PresentationOcular findingsSystemic implication and workup
Amaurosis fugaxTransient monocular vision loss, a "curtain", lasting minutes A transient ischaemic attack of the eye. Same-day or urgent (within 24 hours) stroke workup: carotid imaging, ECG and rhythm monitoring, echocardiography, lipids, glucose, and in anyone over 50 an ESR, CRP, and platelet count for giant cell arteritis. Antiplatelet therapy and risk factor modification. Highest stroke risk is in the first days.
Central retinal artery occlusionSudden painless profound loss, whitened inner retina, cherry red spot, boxcarring, RAPD Treat as an acute stroke: immediate referral to a stroke centre. Look for a Hollenhorst plaque (cholesterol embolus), calcific emboli (cardiac valves, higher mortality), and platelet-fibrin emboli. In anyone over 50 exclude giant cell arteritis urgently.
Branch retinal artery occlusionSectoral whitening and field lossSame embolic workup.
Central retinal vein occlusionDiffuse haemorrhage in all quadrants, dilated tortuous veins, disc oedema, cotton wool spots Hypertension, hyperlipidaemia, diabetes, glaucoma, and hyperviscosity. In a patient under 50, add a thrombophilia and inflammatory screen (antiphospholipid antibodies, homocysteine, protein C and S, factor V Leiden, vasculitis serology, and consider oral contraceptive use). Watch for iris and angle neovascularisation, most likely at 3 to 4 months in ischaemic CRVO.
Branch retinal vein occlusionSectoral haemorrhage respecting the horizontal raphe, at an arteriovenous crossingHypertension is the dominant risk factor.
Ocular ischaemic syndrome Mid-peripheral dot-blot haemorrhages, dilated but not tortuous veins, narrowed arteries, neovascularisation, pain, low IOP despite rubeosis, and reduced retinal artery perfusion pressure ≥90% ipsilateral carotid stenosis. Carries about a 40% five-year mortality, mostly cardiac. Urgent vascular referral.
Giant cell arteritis Arteritic AION with a chalky white swollen disc, or CRAO, or cilioretinal artery occlusion, or diplopia. Amaurosis fugax often precedes. Features: age ≥50 (usually over 70), new headache, scalp tenderness, jaw claudication (the most specific symptom), temporal artery tenderness or reduced pulse, polymyalgia, weight loss, fever, and ESR typically >50 mm/h with a raised CRP and platelets. Start high-dose corticosteroid immediately, before biopsy: intravenous methylprednisolone for visual loss, oral prednisone 1 mg/kg otherwise. Temporal artery biopsy remains valid for up to about two weeks after starting steroid. Untreated, fellow eye involvement occurs in a large proportion within days to weeks. Tocilizumab is steroid-sparing.
Non-arteritic AIONAltitudinal field loss, segmental hyperaemic disc swelling, a crowded "disc at risk" fellow eye Risk factors: hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidaemia, nocturnal hypotension, obstructive sleep apnoea, and PDE5 inhibitors. No proven treatment; manage vascular risk and screen for sleep apnoea. About 15% fellow eye involvement over five years.
Infective endocarditisRoth spots (white-centred haemorrhages), conjunctival petechiae, endogenous endophthalmitis Blood cultures and echocardiography. Roth spots also occur in leukaemia, severe anaemia, diabetes, and hypertension, so they are not specific.
Carotid-cavernous fistulaPulsatile proptosis, orbital bruit, dilated corkscrew episcleral vessels, raised IOP, chemosis, cranial neuropathies High-flow (direct, traumatic) or low-flow (dural). Requires neuroimaging and neurointerventional referral.

Endocrine disease

Thyroid eye disease
  • An autoimmune orbitopathy driven by TSH receptor antibodies stimulating orbital fibroblasts to produce glycosaminoglycans and to differentiate into adipocytes, with lymphocytic infiltration of extraocular muscle bellies (sparing the tendons): the imaging feature that distinguishes it from orbital myositis.
  • Muscle involvement order: "I'M SLow": Inferior rectus, Medial rectus, Superior rectus, Lateral rectus. Inferior rectus fibrosis restricts elevation and mimics a superior rectus palsy.
  • Signs: lid retraction (Dalrymple), lid lag on downgaze (von Graefe), staring appearance (Kocher), infrequent blink (Stellwag), poor convergence (Möbius), proptosis, restrictive strabismus, chemosis, caruncular oedema, superior limbic keratoconjunctivitis, and compressive optic neuropathy at the crowded apex.
  • Activity versus severity: activity is graded by the clinical activity score (pain, redness, swelling), severity by EUGOGO categories or the NOSPECS classification (No signs, Only signs, Soft tissue, Proptosis, Extraocular muscles, Corneal involvement, Sight loss). Treatment targets active disease.
  • Management: stop smoking (the single strongest modifiable risk factor for progression and for failure of treatment), achieve euthyroidism, selenium in mild disease, lubrication and elevation of the head, intravenous methylprednisolone for moderate-to-severe active disease, teprotumumab (anti-IGF-1R; watch for hearing loss and hyperglycaemia), orbital radiotherapy, and surgical decompression urgently for optic neuropathy. Surgical order when needed: decompression, then strabismus, then lid.
  • Radioactive iodine can worsen orbitopathy, so steroid cover is given in at-risk patients.

Sickle cell disease SC and S-thalassaemia produce the most severe proliferative retinopathy, even though SS disease causes more systemic morbidity. Neurologic disease

Neurologic conditions with defining ocular features
ConditionOcular features and management points
Multiple sclerosis Optic neuritis (pain on eye movement, central scotoma, RAPD, red desaturation, a normal or swollen disc, later temporal pallor), internuclear ophthalmoplegia especially bilateral, nystagmus, and Uhthoff phenomenon. ONTT: intravenous methylprednisolone speeds recovery but does not change final acuity; oral prednisone alone at 1 mg/kg is contraindicated because it increased recurrence. MRI with two or more typical white matter lesions raises the 15-year MS risk to about 72% versus 25% with a normal MRI. Consider MOG and aquaporin-4 (neuromyelitis optica) antibodies in severe, bilateral, or steroid-dependent cases, and in children.
Myasthenia gravis Variable, fatigable ptosis and diplopia with a pupil that is always spared. Cogan lid twitch, enhancement of ptosis, improvement with rest or ice pack. Test acetylcholine receptor and MuSK antibodies, single-fibre EMG; image the chest for thymoma. Screen for thyroid disease. Aminoglycosides, fluoroquinolones, macrolides, and β-blockers can worsen it.
Idiopathic intracranial hypertension Bilateral papilloedema, transient visual obscurations, pulsatile tinnitus, headache, and often a sixth nerve palsy; typically an obese young woman. Diagnosis by the modified Dandy criteria with normal imaging (except for empty sella, transverse sinus stenosis, or optic nerve sheath distension) and raised opening pressure. Manage with weight loss, acetazolamide (IIHTT), and surgery (shunt or optic nerve sheath fenestration) for progressive field loss. Perimetry, not acuity, tracks the disease.
Migraine Visual aura with a scintillating, expanding fortification spectrum lasting 20–30 minutes, homonymous and usually followed by headache. Retinal migraine is monocular and requires exclusion of an embolic cause. Acephalgic migraine is common in older patients. A visual aura lasting under 5 or over 60 minutes, or one that is always on the same side, should prompt imaging.
StrokeHomonymous field defects, cortical blindness, gaze palsies, INO, skew deviation, Horner syndrome with Wallenberg syndrome, and visual neglect. Any new homonymous defect is a stroke workup.
Parkinson diseaseReduced blink and dry eye, convergence insufficiency, reduced contrast sensitivity and colour discrimination, apraxia of lid opening, visual hallucinations (also from dopaminergic therapy).
Progressive supranuclear palsyVertical (especially downgaze) supranuclear palsy, square-wave jerks, lid retraction, falls backwards.
Alzheimer diseaseReduced contrast sensitivity, impaired motion and complex visual processing, posterior cortical atrophy variant with visual agnosia and Balint syndrome.
Chiari malformationDownbeat nystagmus, acute acquired comitant esotropia, papilloedema.
Mitochondrial disease Leber hereditary optic neuropathy: mitochondrial (maternally inherited, mtDNA 11778, 3460, 14484), sequential bilateral painless central vision loss in young men, disc telangiectasia without leakage; idebenone and gene therapy are emerging. Chronic progressive external ophthalmoplegia: slowly progressive symmetric ptosis and ophthalmoplegia without diplopia; with pigmentary retinopathy and heart block it becomes Kearns-Sayre syndrome, requiring cardiology referral for pacing. MELAS: mitochondrial encephalopathy, lactic acidosis, and stroke-like episodes with cortical visual loss and pigmentary retinopathy.

Study designs, strongest to weakest for causal inference
DesignStrengths and limitations
Systematic review and meta-analysis of RCTsHighest level; limited by the quality and heterogeneity of included trials and by publication bias.
Randomised controlled trialRandomisation controls confounding, including unknown confounders; masking controls observer and placebo effects. Limited by cost, external validity, and ethics.
Cohort studyProspective, gives incidence and relative risk; vulnerable to loss to follow-up and confounding.
Case-control studyEfficient for rare diseases; gives an odds ratio; vulnerable to recall and selection bias.
Cross-sectional studyGives prevalence; cannot establish temporal sequence.
Case series and case reportHypothesis-generating only.
  • Biases to name: selection, recall, observer (Hawthorne), lead-time and length-time bias in screening, confounding, regression to the mean, attrition, and publication bias.
  • Statistics: a type I error (α) is a false positive conclusion; a type II error (β) is a false negative. Power = 1 − β, conventionally 0.80, and it increases with sample size and effect size. A 95% confidence interval that excludes the null value (0 for a difference, 1 for a ratio) corresponds to p < 0.05; the interval also conveys precision, which a p value does not. Use parametric tests (t test, ANOVA, Pearson) for normally distributed interval data and non-parametric tests (Mann-Whitney, Wilcoxon, Kruskal-Wallis, Spearman) otherwise; chi-square for categorical data.
  • Screening criteria (Wilson and Jungner): the condition must be an important health problem with a recognisable latent stage, a suitable and acceptable test must exist, effective treatment must be available, and the whole programme must be cost-effective and continuous. This framework is why diabetic retinopathy screening is universally endorsed and population glaucoma screening is not.

Retrieval practice
  1. Give the three laboratory thresholds diagnostic of diabetes.
  2. State the 4-2-1 rule and the follow-up interval it triggers.
  3. Define high-risk proliferative diabetic retinopathy in three clauses.
  4. A newly diagnosed type 2 diabetic asks when to have their first eye examination. Answer, and contrast with type 1.
  5. Which retinal finding defines malignant hypertension, and what do you do?
  6. A 74-year-old has sudden vision loss with a chalky white swollen disc and jaw pain on chewing. Give the diagnosis, the three initial tests, and the first action.
  7. Mid-peripheral dot-blot haemorrhages, ocular pain, and rubeosis with a low IOP. Diagnosis, likely stenosis, and prognosis?
  8. Which sickle genotype gives the worst retinopathy, and what drug must you avoid in sickle hyphaema?
  9. Which JIA subtype and serology carries the highest uveitis risk, and how often do you screen?
  10. Give the ANCA type for granulomatosis with polyangiitis and two ocular manifestations.
  11. List the order of extraocular muscle involvement in thyroid eye disease and the single most important modifiable risk factor.
  12. ONTT: what did intravenous steroid change, and what is contraindicated?
  13. Match gene to syndrome: VHL, TSC2, NF2, GNAQ.
  14. Which measures of test performance are independent of prevalence?
  15. A trial reports an event rate of 12% in controls and 8% with treatment. Give ARR, RR, and NNT.
  16. A patient with an easily everted upper lid and chronic irritation. What must you ask about, and why?
Key
  1. Fasting plasma glucose ≥126 mg/dL, 2-hour OGTT ≥200 mg/dL, or HgbA1c ≥6.5% (or a random glucose ≥200 with symptoms).
  2. Severe intraretinal haemorrhages in 4 quadrants, or venous beading in 2 quadrants, or IRMA in 1 quadrant defines severe NPDR; follow every 3 to 4 months.
  3. NVD greater than one third of a disc area; any NVD with vitreous or preretinal haemorrhage; or NVE greater than half a disc area with haemorrhage.
  4. Type 2: dilated examination at diagnosis, because retinopathy may already be present. Type 1: 5 years after diagnosis or at puberty.
  5. Optic disc oedema (grade 4 Keith-Wagener-Barker); send the patient to the emergency department the same day for blood pressure management and end-organ assessment.
  6. Giant cell arteritis with arteritic anterior ischaemic optic neuropathy. Tests: ESR, CRP, and platelet count (then temporal artery biopsy). First action: start high-dose corticosteroid immediately, before the biopsy.
  7. Ocular ischaemic syndrome; ipsilateral carotid stenosis of at least 90%; roughly 40% five-year mortality, mostly cardiovascular, so urgent vascular referral.
  8. SC and S-thalassaemia genotypes; avoid carbonic anhydrase inhibitors, which cause systemic acidosis and promote sickling in the anterior chamber.
  9. Oligoarticular, ANA-positive young girls with early onset; screen every 3 months.
  10. c-ANCA / anti-PR3; necrotising scleritis and orbital inflammatory mass with bone destruction (also peripheral ulcerative keratitis and nasolacrimal obstruction).
  11. Inferior rectus, medial rectus, superior rectus, lateral rectus ("I'M SLow"); smoking cessation.
  12. Intravenous methylprednisolone accelerated visual recovery but did not change final acuity; oral prednisone alone at 1 mg/kg is contraindicated because it increased the recurrence rate.
  13. VHL: von Hippel-Lindau. TSC2: tuberous sclerosis. NF2: neurofibromatosis 2. GNAQ: Sturge-Weber (somatic mosaic).
  14. Sensitivity, specificity, and both likelihood ratios. Predictive values depend on prevalence.
  15. ARR = 12% − 8% = 4%. RR = 8/12 = 0.67 (a 33% relative risk reduction). NNT = 1/0.04 = 25.
  16. Ask about snoring and daytime somnolence: floppy eyelid syndrome is strongly associated with obstructive sleep apnoea, which itself carries cardiovascular, NAION, and glaucoma risk.
Chapter recap
  1. Diabetes: three diagnostic thresholds, 4-2-1 for severe NPDR, high-risk PDR triggers treatment, anti-VEGF first for centre-involving oedema.
  2. Type 2 screened at diagnosis, type 1 at five years, pregnancy each trimester.
  3. Hypertensive grade 4 means disc oedema and an emergency department the same day.
  4. Amaurosis fugax and CRAO are strokes; over 50, always exclude giant cell arteritis before anything else.
  5. Thyroid eye disease spares tendons, follows I'M SLow, and smoking is the modifiable driver.
  6. SC sickle disease gives the worst retinopathy; never give a carbonic anhydrase inhibitor in sickle hyphaema.
  7. ANA-positive oligoarticular JIA has a white, painless, sight-threatening uveitis screened three-monthly.
  8. c-ANCA for GPA, HLA-B51 for Behçet, HLA-A29 for birdshot, DR4 for VKH.
  9. Phakomatoses map one-to-one to genes and to a signature ocular lesion.
  10. Sensitivity and specificity are prevalence-independent; predictive values are not. NNT = 1/ARR.
