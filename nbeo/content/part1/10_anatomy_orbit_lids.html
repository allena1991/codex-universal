The bony orbit

The orbit is a four-walled pyramid whose apex points posteromedially. Its volume is ~30 mL, of which the globe occupies only about 6.5 mL. Orbital depth from rim to apex is 40–45 mm; the entrance measures roughly 35 mm high by 40–45 mm wide, with the widest point about 1 cm behind the anterior rim. The medial walls are essentially parallel to one another and to the sagittal plane; the lateral walls diverge at about 45° from the medial wall, so the two orbital axes diverge ~45° from each other and each axis sits ~23° from the sagittal plane.

Seven bones of the orbit

"Many Friendly Zebras Enjoyed Lazy Sunday Picnics"

Maxillary, Frontal, Zygomatic, Ethmoid, Lacrimal, Sphenoid, Palatine. Only the palatine contributes no part of a named wall boundary you will be asked to draw: it forms a small posteromedial piece of the floor.

Thinnest versus weakest

The medial wall is the thinnest bone, but the floor is the wall that most commonly fractures in a blowout, because the medial wall is buttressed by the ethmoidal air-cell septa while the floor is unsupported over the maxillary sinus. Both statements are true and the exam uses one to distract from the other.

The annulus of Zinn distinction is a favourite item

The annulus (anulus) of Zinn is the common tendinous ring from which the four rectus muscles originate at the apex. Nerves destined for muscles inside the muscle cone (III, VI, and the sensory nasociliary) pass inside the ring; the trochlear nerve enters outside it, which is consistent with the superior oblique lying outside the cone. Frontal and lacrimal nerves and the superior ophthalmic vein also stay outside.

Orbital apex syndrome = superior orbital fissure syndrome plus optic nerve involvement: ophthalmoplegia (III, IV, VI), V1 anaesthesia, and reduced vision with an afferent defect. Superior orbital fissure syndrome spares the optic nerve.

Orbital soft tissue architecture

Periorbita
Periosteum lining the orbital bones, loosely adherent except at sutures, foramina, and the orbital rim. Its loose attachment is why subperiosteal abscess and haematoma can dissect widely.
Orbital septum
Fibrous sheet from the arcus marginalis at the orbital rim to the levator aponeurosis (upper, 2–5 mm above the tarsus) and to the inferior tarsal border (lower). It is the anatomic barrier that separates preseptal from orbital cellulitis, the single most consequential anatomic boundary in lid disease.
Tenon capsule (fascia bulbi)
Dense fibroelastic envelope from the limbus to the optic nerve, separated from the sclera by the episcleral space. Fuses with the intermuscular septum and is pierced by the EOM tendons ~10 mm behind their insertions. Preserving it prevents fat prolapse and adhesive restriction after strabismus surgery.
Check ligaments
Extensions of the medial rectus sheath to the lacrimal bone and the lateral rectus sheath to Whitnall tubercle; they limit excursion.
Lockwood ligament
Suspensory hammock beneath the globe formed by the fused inferior rectus and inferior oblique sheaths; it prevents the globe sinking after maxillectomy.
Whitnall ligament
Superior transverse ligament of the levator: the fulcrum where the levator's horizontal vector turns vertical. Sits ~10–12 mm above the superior tarsal border.
Orbital fat
Intraconal (central) and extraconal (peripheral) compartments, separated by the intermuscular septum. Intraconal fat expansion drives axial proptosis; the medial extraconal fat pad is the one removed in decompression.

The eyelids

Dimensions and landmarks

  • Palpebral fissure: 8–11 mm vertically, 28–30 mm horizontally in the adult. Upper lid margin normally sits 1–2 mm below the superior limbus; lower lid rests at the inferior limbus.
  • Marginal reflex distance 1 (MRD1), pupil centre to upper lid margin: 4–5 mm. MRD2, pupil centre to lower lid margin: 5–5.5 mm.
  • Upper lid crease: 8–11 mm above the margin in Caucasian lids, 3–6 mm or absent in East Asian lids because the levator aponeurosis inserts lower and preaponeurotic fat descends further.
  • Tarsal plates: dense connective tissue (not cartilage). Upper 10–11 mm vertical height, lower 4–5 mm, both about 1 mm thick and 29 mm long.
  • Lashes: ~100–150 in two to three irregular rows on the upper lid, ~50–75 in one to two rows on the lower. No lashes medial to the punctum. Lifespan 3–5 months; they do not grey with age at the same rate as scalp hair.
  • Gray line: the visible line on the lid margin corresponding to the muscle of Riolan (the most superficial pretarsal orbicularis). It marks the surgical plane separating anterior lamella (skin, orbicularis) from posterior lamella (tarsus, conjunctiva).
  • Meibomian orifices lie just anterior to the mucocutaneous junction and posterior to the gray line.

Layers of the upper lid, superficial to deep

Seven layers, in order
  1. Skin: the thinnest in the body, <1 mm, with no subcutaneous fat over the pretarsal region.
  2. Subcutaneous areolar tissue: loose, which is why oedema and blood accumulate dramatically here.
  3. Orbicularis oculi (CN VII): pretarsal, preseptal, and orbital portions. Pretarsal and preseptal heads form the lacrimal pump; the orbital portion produces forced closure.
  4. Orbital septum, with preaponeurotic (postseptal) fat immediately behind it. Fat behind the septum is the surgical landmark for the levator: the levator aponeurosis lies directly deep to the preaponeurotic fat pad.
  5. Levator palpebrae superioris aponeurosis, with its medial and lateral horns.
  6. Müller muscle (superior tarsal muscle): smooth muscle, sympathetically innervated, from the undersurface of the levator to the superior tarsal border, contributing ~2 mm of lid elevation.
  7. Palpebral conjunctiva: firmly adherent to the tarsus, non-keratinised stratified squamous with goblet cells.

The lower lid mirrors this: skin, orbicularis, septum, capsulopalpebral fascia (the inferior rectus analogue of the levator aponeurosis), the inferior tarsal muscle (sympathetic Müller analogue), tarsus, and conjunctiva. Disinsertion of the capsulopalpebral fascia produces involutional entropion.

Ptosis localisation follows the anatomy
  • CN III palsy: levator denervated, ptosis is severe (>4 mm), often with pupil involvement and ophthalmoplegia.
  • Horner syndrome: Müller denervated, ptosis is mild (1–2 mm), with reverse ptosis of the lower lid from inferior tarsal muscle loss, miosis, and anhidrosis. Apparent enophthalmos is an illusion created by the narrowed fissure.
  • Aponeurotic (involutional) ptosis: levator function is normal, the aponeurosis has stretched. High crease, good excursion, deep superior sulcus.
  • Myasthenia gravis: variable, fatigable ptosis with Cogan lid twitch; pupil is always spared.

Which gland, which cyst

Moll is Moist (sweat, apocrine, clear cyst = hidrocystoma). Zeis is Zebaceous (oil on the lash, stye). Meibomian is Meaty and lives in the tarsus (chalazion).

Eyelid vasculature and its clinical danger

The lids receive a double supply, which is why lid flaps survive well.

  • From the internal carotid via the ophthalmic artery: supraorbital, supratrochlear, dorsal nasal, lacrimal, and the medial palpebral arteries.
  • From the external carotid via the facial artery (angular artery), superficial temporal artery (lateral palpebral, transverse facial), and infraorbital artery.
  • These anastomose in the marginal arcade (in the pretarsal plane 2–4 mm from the margin) and the peripheral arcade (at the upper tarsal border, between levator aponeurosis and Müller muscle).
Why lid cellulitis is not a trivial diagnosis

Venous drainage runs medially through the angular vein to the superior ophthalmic vein and then to the cavernous sinus. The orbital and facial veins are valveless, so flow can reverse. This is the anatomic basis of the "danger triangle" of the face and of cavernous sinus thrombosis complicating a medial canthal or nasal infection. Bilateral involvement, proptosis, chemosis, and multiple cranial neuropathies (III, IV, V1, V2, VI) signal that the infection has crossed into the sinus.

Hutchinson sign

Herpes zoster vesicles on the tip, side, or root of the nose indicate involvement of the nasociliary nerve. Because the nasociliary also supplies the globe through the long ciliary nerves, this predicts a substantially higher risk of zoster keratitis and iritis. The rest of V1 dermatomal involvement does not carry the same predictive weight.

The lacrimal system

Secretory apparatus

The main lacrimal gland sits in the lacrimal fossa of the frontal bone in the superotemporal orbit. The lateral horn of the levator aponeurosis divides it into a larger orbital lobe and a smaller palpebral lobe, the latter visible through the superior temporal fornix on lid eversion. About 12 ductules drain the orbital lobe through the palpebral lobe into the superior temporal fornix.

Surgical corollary

Because the orbital lobe's ducts traverse the palpebral lobe, biopsy or excision of the palpebral lobe can de-function the entire gland. Biopsy is therefore taken from the orbital lobe when tissue is needed.

Parasympathetic pathway to the lacrimal gland: memorise this chain
  1. Lacrimal (superior salivatory) nucleus in the pons.
  2. Fibres leave with the nervus intermedius of CN VII.
  3. Branch off at the geniculate ganglion as the greater superficial petrosal nerve.
  4. Joins the deep petrosal (sympathetic) to become the nerve of the pterygoid canal (vidian nerve).
  5. Synapse in the pterygopalatine (sphenopalatine) ganglion.
  6. Postganglionic fibres travel with the zygomatic nerve (V2), then its zygomaticotemporal branch, and join the lacrimal nerve (V1) to reach the gland.

Sympathetic supply is from the superior cervical ganglion along the internal carotid plexus, reaching the gland via the deep petrosal nerve and the lacrimal artery. Sensory supply is the lacrimal nerve (V1). Arterial supply is the lacrimal artery from the ophthalmic artery.

Crocodile tears and the vidian nerve

After a proximal facial nerve injury, regenerating salivatory fibres may misroute into the lacrimal pathway, producing gustatory lacrimation: tearing when eating. The shared pterygopalatine relay is the reason this aberrant regeneration is possible.

The lacrimal pump (Jones mechanism)

Contraction of the pretarsal orbicularis shortens the canaliculi and compresses the ampullae while the preseptal orbicularis (Horner muscle) pulls the sac's posterolateral wall to create negative pressure; on relaxation, elastic recoil plus gravity and capillary force move tears into the duct. This is why orbicularis weakness (CN VII palsy) causes epiphora even with a patent system, and why lower lid laxity or ectropion produces a functional obstruction with normal irrigation.

Roughly 10–25% of tear volume is lost to evaporation; the remainder drains, with the lower canaliculus historically credited with the larger share, though both contribute substantially.

Two mnemonics that carry a lot of weight

LR6SO4, all the rest CN III. Lateral rectus is CN VI, superior oblique is CN IV.

Spiral of Tillaux: 5–6–7–8 (approximately) for Medial 5.5, Inferior 6.5, Lateral 6.9, Superior 7.7 mm from the limbus. Read it as "MILS", nasal to superior, increasing distance.

"Obliques do the opposite": the obliques abduct while the vertical recti adduct; the superior muscles intort and the inferior muscles extort (Superiors are Intorters: "SIN" and "RAID": Recti ADduct, obliques abduct).

Why the field of gaze changes muscle action

The vertical recti are most efficient elevators/depressors in abduction (their line of pull aligns with the visual axis at 23° of abduction). The obliques are most efficient elevators/depressors in adduction (aligning at 51° of adduction). This is the entire logic of the H-pattern / cardinal positions test and of the three-step (Parks-Bielschowsky) test.

In torsion the obliques dominate: SO intorts, IO extorts, and they do so maximally in abduction, which is why a superior oblique palsy shows worsening hypertropia on ipsilateral head tilt.

Muscle structure and blood supply

  • EOMs are among the fastest and most fatigue-resistant striated muscles in the body, with the highest ratio of nerve fibres to muscle fibres (about 1:3–1:5 versus 1:100+ in limb muscle), permitting exquisitely graded movement.
  • Two layers: an orbital layer (outer, inserting on the pulley connective tissue, entirely fatigue-resistant) and a global layer (inner, inserting on the sclera). Fibre types include singly innervated fast twitch and multiply innervated slow tonic fibres, the latter unusual outside the EOMs.
  • Arterial supply: the superior and inferior muscular branches of the ophthalmic artery. The lateral rectus is supplied by the lacrimal artery, and the inferior oblique also receives infraorbital contributions.
  • Anterior ciliary arteries arise from the muscular branches and run in the rectus tendons to the limbus: two each from the medial, superior, and inferior recti and one from the lateral rectus, for seven total. They supply the anterior segment. This is why detaching three or more rectus muscles in one sitting risks anterior segment ischaemia.
  • The vortex veins (four to eight, one to two per quadrant) exit near the equator and drain the uvea into the superior and inferior ophthalmic veins.
  • Levator palpebrae superioris, though a lid muscle, originates at the apex above the annulus and is supplied by CN III superior division along with the superior rectus, which is why ptosis and elevation deficit travel together.

Key
  1. Orbital plate of the frontal bone and lesser wing of the sphenoid; the anterior cranial fossa (and frontal sinus anteriorly).
  2. Orbital floor blowout (maxillary sinus, entrapment of inferior rectus/inferior oblique on upgaze) and medial wall (lamina papyracea, emphysema); the infraorbital nerve, V2.
  3. CN IV, frontal nerve, lacrimal nerve, and the superior ophthalmic vein.
  4. Horner syndrome, an oculosympathetic lesion; Müller (superior tarsal) muscle, with the inferior tarsal muscle producing the reverse ptosis.
  5. Pterygopalatine (sphenopalatine) ganglion; zygomatic branch of V2 (via zygomaticotemporal) joining the lacrimal nerve of V1.
  6. Into the inferior nasal meatus; the valve of Hasner.
  7. The orbicularis-driven lacrimal pump fails, so tears are not actively propelled; the anatomic lumen is open but the pump is not working.
  8. Inferior oblique, from the maxilla near the nasolacrimal canal; primary action is extorsion.
  9. Seven; the lateral rectus.
  10. In abduction of about 23°, where its line of pull aligns with the visual axis so its entire force becomes depression.
Chapter recap: expand each line into a paragraph
  1. Seven bones, four walls, and one rule: thinnest is medial, weakest is the posteromedial floor, strongest is lateral.
  2. The annulus of Zinn sorts the apex: III, VI, nasociliary, sympathetics inside; IV, frontal, lacrimal, superior ophthalmic vein outside.
  3. The orbital septum divides preseptal from orbital cellulitis; the gray line divides anterior from posterior lamella.
  4. Levator is CN III and gives gross ptosis; Müller is sympathetic and gives 1 to 2 mm with reverse ptosis.
  5. Gland to lesion: Meibomian to chalazion, Zeis to stye, Moll to hidrocystoma, goblet cell loss to keratinisation.
  6. Valveless facial and orbital veins connect the medial canthus to the cavernous sinus.
  7. Lacrimal parasympathetics: pons to VII to greater petrosal to vidian to pterygopalatine to V2 to lacrimal nerve.
  8. Tear drainage: punctum, ampulla, canaliculus, Rosenmüller, sac, duct, Hasner, inferior meatus, and an orbicularis pump that must work.
  9. Spiral of Tillaux 5.5 / 6.5 / 6.9 / 7.7; LR6SO4; recti adduct and obliques abduct; superiors intort.
  10. Seven anterior ciliary arteries mean three-muscle surgery risks anterior segment ischaemia.
