The optic nerve: four segments

Optic nerve anatomy by segment
SegmentLengthFeatures and clinical consequence
Intraocular~1 mm The disc and the lamina cribrosa (fenestrated scleral collagen). Axons are unmyelinated here, which is why the nerve narrows from ~3 mm in diameter behind the sclera to 1.5 mm at the disc. Backward bowing of the lamina under pressure is the structural event in glaucomatous cupping.
Intraorbital25–30 mm Runs an S-shaped, slack course in a distance of only about 18 mm: the redundancy permits ~9 mm of proptosis before traction. Surrounded by pia, arachnoid, and dura continuous with the intracranial meninges, so the subarachnoid space communicates with the intracranial CSF space. That single fact produces papilloedema in raised intracranial pressure and permits optic nerve sheath fenestration as a treatment.
Intracanalicular4–10 mm Tethered to the periosteum within the optic canal, with the ophthalmic artery inferolateral. Deceleration injury transmits shear here: this is the site of traumatic optic neuropathy. Also the site compressed by sphenoid ridge meningioma and fibrous dysplasia.
Intracranial10–16 mm From the canal to the chiasm, lying above the cavernous sinus and beside the internal carotid and anterior cerebral arteries. Loses the dural sheath, gaining only pia. Compression here by an olfactory groove or tuberculum sellae meningioma gives a junctional scotoma.
The optic nerve is a central nervous system tract, not a peripheral nerve
  • Myelination is by oligodendrocytes and begins immediately posterior to the lamina cribrosa. There are no Schwann cells and no endoneurium.
  • Consequence 1: it does not regenerate.
  • Consequence 2: it is a target of central demyelination, so optic neuritis is an MS phenomenon and not a Guillain-Barré phenomenon.
  • Consequence 3: myelinated retinal nerve fibres represent oligodendrocytes that migrated anterior to the lamina, appearing as feathery white patches obscuring vessels.
  • Consequence 4: the nerve has meninges, so it has a subarachnoid space, so intracranial pressure is transmitted to the disc.

Chiasm relationships

  • Inferior: the pituitary gland and sella turcica, separated by the diaphragma sellae. A pituitary adenoma grows upward and compresses the inferior chiasmal fibres first, which carry the superior visual field, so the classic evolution is a bitemporal superior quadrantanopia progressing to bitemporal hemianopia.
  • Superior: the third ventricle floor and hypothalamus. A craniopharyngioma compresses from above, taking the inferior field first.
  • Lateral: the internal carotid arteries and the cavernous sinuses.
  • Anterior: the anterior cerebral arteries and anterior communicating artery.
  • Position relative to the sella is variable: pre-fixed (anterior, ~15%), normal (~80%), post-fixed (~5%). This variability is why identical tumours produce different fields.

Congruity is a localising sign

The further posterior the lesion, the more congruous (identical in the two eyes) the homonymous defect, because corresponding fibres from the two eyes converge progressively toward the cortex. Optic tract lesions are markedly incongruous; occipital lesions are exquisitely congruous. Congruity is worth more than any other single feature in localising a homonymous defect.

Cranial nerves of the orbit

CN III: oculomotor

  • Nuclei in the midbrain at the level of the superior colliculus: a paired somatic motor complex plus the Edinger-Westphal nucleus supplying preganglionic parasympathetics.
  • Fibres pass ventrally through the red nucleus and cerebral peduncle. Combined lesions here are named: Nothnagel (III + cerebellar ataxia), Benedikt (III + red nucleus, contralateral tremor), Weber (III + corticospinal tract, contralateral hemiparesis), Claude (Benedikt plus Nothnagel features).
  • Exits into the interpeduncular fossa between the posterior cerebral and superior cerebellar arteries, then runs beside the posterior communicating artery: a PCom aneurysm is the classic cause of a painful, pupil-involving palsy.
  • Passes through the lateral wall of the cavernous sinus, then the superior orbital fissure inside the annulus of Zinn, dividing into:
    • Superior division: superior rectus and levator palpebrae superioris.
    • Inferior division: medial rectus, inferior rectus, inferior oblique, and the preganglionic parasympathetic fibres to the ciliary ganglion (branching from the nerve to the inferior oblique).
The rule of the pupil, and its anatomic reason

Parasympathetic pupillomotor fibres travel superficially and dorsomedially in the nerve, and they are supplied by pial vessels. Somatic motor fibres run in the core, supplied by intraneural vasa nervorum.

  • Compression (aneurysm, uncal herniation, tumour) squeezes the outside first, so the pupil is involved, often before or with the motility deficit. This is a neurosurgical emergency requiring same-day vascular imaging.
  • Microvascular ischaemia (diabetes, hypertension) infarcts the core, so the pupil is spared and the palsy is painful but benign, usually recovering in 8–12 weeks.
  • Aberrant regeneration (lid elevation on adduction or downgaze, pupil constriction on adduction) implies a slowly compressive lesion: cavernous meningioma, aneurysm, or trauma. It never follows an ischaemic palsy.

CN IV: trochlear

  • Nucleus in the midbrain at the level of the inferior colliculus.
  • The only cranial nerve to decussate completely and the only one to exit the brainstem dorsally, just below the inferior colliculus. Therefore a nuclear fourth nerve lesion causes a contralateral superior oblique palsy while a lesion anywhere along the nerve causes an ipsilateral palsy.
  • Longest intracranial course (~75 mm) and thinnest nerve, wrapping around the brainstem, passing between PCA and SCA, then along the lateral cavernous sinus wall and through the superior orbital fissure outside the annulus of Zinn.
  • Its dorsal exit and long course make it the nerve most often injured in closed head trauma, frequently bilaterally.

CN VI: abducens

  • Nucleus in the pons, beneath the facial colliculus, because the motor root of CN VII loops around it. Therefore a nuclear sixth nerve lesion gives an ipsilateral horizontal gaze palsy (not an isolated abduction deficit, because the nucleus also contains interneurons projecting via the MLF to the contralateral medial rectus subnucleus) plus an ipsilateral lower motor neuron facial palsy. That pairing localises the lesion to the pons with near certainty.
  • Long subarachnoid course, ascends the petrous ridge and passes through Dorello canal under the petroclinoid (Gruber) ligament: this fixation makes it the classic false localising sign in raised intracranial pressure and the nerve affected in Gradenigo syndrome (petrous apicitis, with VI palsy, facial pain, and otorrhoea).
  • Runs inside the cavernous sinus, adjacent to the internal carotid artery, not in the lateral wall. It is therefore the nerve most often affected first by an intracavernous carotid aneurysm or carotid-cavernous fistula.
  • Millard-Gubler syndrome: ventral pontine lesion with VI and VII palsies plus contralateral hemiplegia. Foville syndrome: dorsal pontine, with gaze palsy, VII palsy, and Horner syndrome.

  • CN VII has a motor root to the muscles of facial expression, including orbicularis oculi (efferent limb of the blink and corneal reflex), and the nervus intermedius carrying taste and the parasympathetic secretomotor fibres to the lacrimal, submandibular, and sublingual glands.

A supranuclear (upper motor neuron) lesion therefore spares the forehead and permits lid closure; a nuclear or peripheral lesion causes complete hemifacial weakness with lagophthalmos. This is the single most useful clinical discriminator in facial weakness, and it determines whether corneal exposure is a risk.

The ciliary ganglion

Three roots, one synapse, and a 30-to-1 ratio

The ciliary ganglion lies in the posterior orbit, roughly 1 cm anterior to the superior orbital fissure, lateral to the optic nerve and medial to the lateral rectus.

RootOriginSynapse?
Parasympathetic (motor)Preganglionic from Edinger-Westphal via CN III inferior divisionYes. The only root that synapses.
SensoryNasociliary nerve (V1)No, passes through.
SympatheticInternal carotid plexus, from the superior cervical ganglionNo, passes through.

Postganglionic fibres leave as 6–10 short ciliary nerves. Crucially, about 97% supply the ciliary muscle and only 3% supply the iris sphincter. That ratio explains two clinical phenomena: after ganglion damage, aberrantly regenerating accommodative fibres readily reinnervate the sphincter, giving light-near dissociation; and it explains the sectoral, vermiform movements of a tonic (Adie) pupil.

The pupil

Light reflex: four neurons
  1. Retina to pretectum. Photoreceptors and intrinsically photosensitive ganglion cells → optic nerve → chiasm (nasal fibres cross) → optic tract → fibres leave before the LGN via the brachium of the superior colliculus → pretectal olivary nucleus.
  2. Pretectum to both Edinger-Westphal nuclei, crossing in the posterior commissure. This double decussation (chiasm and posterior commissure) is why the reflex is consensual and symmetric.
  3. Edinger-Westphal to ciliary ganglion via CN III inferior division.
  4. Ciliary ganglion to sphincter via the short ciliary nerves.

The near reflex (accommodation, convergence, miosis) is driven from the occipital association cortex to the Edinger-Westphal nucleus without a pretectal relay, and the fibres approach the nucleus more ventrally.

The oculosympathetic pathway and Horner syndrome

Three neurons, three lesion levels
  1. First order (central): posterolateral hypothalamus → descends uncrossed through the brainstem and cervical cord to the ciliospinal centre of Budge-Waller at C8 to T2. Lesions: brainstem stroke (lateral medullary/Wallenberg), demyelination, syringomyelia. Look for anhidrosis of the whole ipsilateral body and other brainstem signs.
  2. Second order (preganglionic): exits the cord, crosses the apex of the lung and the subclavian artery, ascends the sympathetic chain to the superior cervical ganglion. Lesions: Pancoast tumour, thyroid or neck surgery, brachial plexus trauma, aortic or subclavian aneurysm, and in children neuroblastoma. Anhidrosis of the face and neck.
  3. Third order (postganglionic): travels on the internal carotid artery plexus, enters the cavernous sinus, joins V1, then the nasociliary and long ciliary nerves to the dilator pupillae and Müller muscle. Lesions: carotid dissection (painful Horner is a dissection until proven otherwise), cavernous sinus disease, cluster headache (Raeder paratrigeminal syndrome). Little or no anhidrosis, since sudomotor fibres leave with the external carotid.

Cocaine 4–10%Blocks noradrenaline reuptake, so dilation requires intact tonic release Confirms Horner syndrome at any level. The Horner pupil fails to dilate; anisocoria increases. Positive if post-drop anisocoria is ≥0.8–1.0 mm.

Internuclear ophthalmoplegia and one-and-a-half syndrome

Internuclear ophthalmoplegia (INO) = a lesion of the medial longitudinal fasciculus. On attempted gaze away from the lesion, the ipsilateral eye fails to adduct while the contralateral abducting eye shows nystagmus. Convergence is preserved if the lesion is caudal to the CN III nucleus. Bilateral INO in a young adult means multiple sclerosis until proven otherwise; in an older patient, pontine infarct. Bilateral INO with exotropia is WEBINO.

One-and-a-half syndrome = ipsilateral PPRF (or CN VI nucleus) plus the adjacent MLF. The patient has a complete ipsilateral horizontal gaze palsy (the "one") and, on gaze to the other side, only abduction of the contralateral eye (the "half"), because the ipsilateral eye cannot adduct. The only remaining horizontal movement is abduction of the contralateral eye.

Cavernous sinus syndrome: any combination of III, IV, V1, V2, VI, and a Horner syndrome, with proptosis and chemosis. Because both sympathetic and parasympathetic pupillomotor supply can be interrupted, the pupil may be mid-sized and unreactive. A painful multiple ophthalmoplegia with V1 numbness and normal imaging suggests Tolosa-Hunt syndrome. Pulsatile proptosis with an orbital bruit, dilated "corkscrew" episcleral vessels, and raised IOP suggests a carotid-cavernous fistula.

Key
  1. Immediately posterior to the lamina cribrosa, by oligodendrocytes. Consequences: no regeneration; it is a CNS tract subject to central demyelination; and myelinated nerve fibres represent anterior oligodendrocyte migration.
  2. Bitemporal superior quadrantanopia, because the tumour grows upward from the sella and compresses the inferior chiasmal fibres, which carry the superior field.
  3. Temporal = "pie in the sky" superior quadrantanopia from Meyer loop, often with complex partial seizures or formed hallucinations. Parietal = "pie on the floor" inferior quadrantanopia, often with hemineglect and an asymmetric optokinetic response toward the lesion.
  4. The occipital pole receives collateral supply from the middle cerebral artery.
  5. Layers 1 and 2 are magnocellular; crossed (contralateral) fibres go to layers 1, 4, and 6.
  6. Pupillomotor fibres run superficially and dorsomedially and are pially supplied, so external compression reaches them first; motor fibres in the core are spared until later.
  7. It indicates a slowly compressive or traumatic lesion, and effectively excludes microvascular ischaemia.
  8. The pons, at the CN VI nucleus beneath the facial colliculus, where the facial motor root loops around it.
  9. CN IV; a nuclear lesion causes a contralateral superior oblique palsy.
  10. CN VI, adjacent to the internal carotid artery.
  11. Parasympathetic (from CN III inferior division) synapses; sensory (nasociliary) and sympathetic (carotid plexus) pass through without synapsing.
  12. Cocaine or apraclonidine confirms Horner at any level; hydroxyamphetamine then localises, dilating in first- and second-order lesions and failing in third-order.
  13. Ipsilateral complete horizontal gaze palsy plus loss of adduction of the ipsilateral eye on contralateral gaze; the PPRF (or CN VI nucleus) and the adjacent MLF.
  14. Dorsal midbrain (Parinaud) syndrome, Argyll Robertson pupils, Adie tonic pupil, severe bilateral afferent disease (also aberrant CN III regeneration and diabetic autonomic neuropathy).
Chapter recap
  1. Four ON segments; 1 mm intraocular, 25 to 30 mm orbital with slack, 4 to 10 mm tethered in the canal, 10 to 16 mm intracranial.
  2. It is a CNS tract with meninges: no regeneration, central demyelination, papilloedema.
  3. Inferior chiasmal fibres carry the superior field; that is why pituitary tumours start superotemporally.
  4. Inferior radiations loop through the temporal lobe (Meyer) for the superior field; congruity increases posteriorly.
  5. LGN: 1 and 2 magno; crossed to 1, 4, 6.
  6. Pupil fibres are superficial: compression involves the pupil, ischaemia spares it, aberrant regeneration means compression.
  7. Nuclear IV is contralateral; nuclear VI gives gaze palsy plus facial palsy; VI runs inside the cavernous sinus.
  8. Ciliary ganglion: only the parasympathetic root synapses, and 97% of output drives accommodation.
  9. Horner: three neurons, three drugs, and painful Horner means dissection.
  10. INO is an MLF lesion; add the PPRF and it becomes one-and-a-half.
