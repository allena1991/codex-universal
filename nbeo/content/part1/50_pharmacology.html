Principles and ocular drug delivery

Pharmacologic vocabulary that appears in stems
TermDefinition
PotencyThe dose required for a given effect (position of the dose-response curve on the x-axis). Quantified by ED50.
EfficacyThe maximum achievable effect (height of the curve). A less potent drug can be more efficacious.
Therapeutic indexLD50 / ED50. A narrow index means small dosing errors matter (digoxin, warfarin, lithium, aminoglycosides).
Competitive antagonistShifts the dose-response curve right, surmountable by more agonist; reduces potency, not efficacy.
Non-competitive antagonistReduces maximum response; not surmountable.
Partial agonistSubmaximal effect even at receptor saturation; acts as an antagonist in the presence of a full agonist (carteolol has intrinsic sympathomimetic activity on this basis).
TachyphylaxisRapid loss of response with repeated dosing (apraclonidine is the classic ocular example).
ProdrugInactive until metabolised: dipivefrin to epinephrine, latanoprost to the free acid, valacyclovir to aciclovir. Used to improve corneal or gastrointestinal penetration.
First-pass effectHepatic metabolism of an orally absorbed drug before systemic circulation; bypassed by topical, sublingual, and nasal routes, which is why topical timolol can produce systemic β-blockade.
Why so little topical drug gets in, and what to do about it
  • A commercial drop is 35–50 µL; the tear film holds 7–9 µL and the cul-de-sac can accept at most 25–30 µL. Most of the drop is lost immediately.
  • Tear turnover of ~16%/min clears the rest within minutes. Net corneal bioavailability is typically <5%, and often 1–2%.
  • The remainder drains through the nasolacrimal system and is absorbed across highly vascular nasal mucosa, bypassing hepatic first pass. This is the route of systemic β-blocker toxicity, of tachycardia from phenylephrine, and of CNS depression from brimonidine in infants.
  • To increase ocular effect and reduce systemic effect: instil one drop, not two; occlude the puncta or close the lids gently for 2 to 3 minutes; space different drugs by 5 minutes; use gels, ointments, or viscosity agents to prolong contact; and use the lowest effective concentration.
  • Corneal penetration requires biphasic solubility because the epithelium is lipophilic and the stroma hydrophilic. Benzalkonium chloride increases penetration by disrupting epithelial tight junctions, which is also why it causes surface toxicity with chronic use and why preservative-free formulations exist for glaucoma and dry eye patients on multiple drops.
  • Inflamed and abraded eyes absorb far more drug: the barrier is broken. Dose accordingly and expect greater systemic exposure.

Dilation risk, and the two questions that actually matter

The risk of precipitating angle closure with routine dilation is very low (well under 1 in 1000). The relevant assessments are the van Herick or gonioscopic angle grade and the presence of prior symptoms, not a history of "glaucoma", since open-angle glaucoma is not a contraindication. Never withhold a dilated fundus examination from a diabetic or a patient with flashes and floaters because of unfounded closure anxiety; assess the angle, then dilate.

Genuine cautions: a very narrow or occludable angle without a patent iridotomy; known plateau iris; an anteriorly subluxated lens; an iris-supported anterior chamber IOL; and cardiac disease with phenylephrine 10%.

  • Mechanism: reversible blockade of voltage-gated sodium channels from the cytoplasmic side, preventing depolarisation. The unionised form crosses the membrane, the ionised form blocks, which is why anaesthetics work poorly in acidic inflamed tissue.
  • Esters are metabolised by plasma pseudocholinesterase to PABA, the allergen in the rare true anaesthetic allergy; amides are metabolised hepatically and do not produce PABA. Remember the mnemonic that amides contain an "i" in the prefix (lidocaine, bupivacaine, mepivacaine).
  • Never dispense topical anaesthetics. Repeated use produces epithelial toxicity, delayed healing, neurotrophic ulceration, ring infiltrates, and permanent scarring. This is an examinable professional standard, not merely a caution.

Acute primary angle closure: the sequence
  1. Topical aqueous suppressants immediately: a β-blocker, an α2-agonist, and a topical carbonic anhydrase inhibitor, one drop each.
  2. Oral or intravenous acetazolamide (500 mg), assuming no sickle cell disease or sulfa allergy.
  3. Topical steroid to reduce inflammation and improve corneal clarity.
  4. Pilocarpine 1–2% only once IOP has fallen below roughly 40–50 mmHg, because a severely ischaemic iris sphincter will not respond and pilocarpine can worsen the situation by increasing pupillary block through anterior lens movement.
  5. Corneal indentation (with a four-mirror goniolens) to force aqueous into the angle, and supine positioning to let the lens fall back.
  6. Hyperosmotic agent if still uncontrolled.
  7. Definitive treatment: laser peripheral iridotomy in the affected eye and prophylactically in the fellow eye, because the fellow eye has roughly a 50% risk over five years.

Steroid mechanism and the four consequences you must monitor

Mechanism: glucocorticoids induce lipocortin (annexin A1), which inhibits phospholipase A2, blocking arachidonic acid release and therefore both the cyclooxygenase (prostaglandins) and lipoxygenase (leukotrienes) pathways. NSAIDs block only cyclooxygenase, which is why steroids are broader and why NSAIDs never fully substitute.

  1. Raised IOP. About 5% of the general population are high responders (a rise of more than 15 mmHg) and roughly a third are moderate responders; the rate is much higher in patients with primary open-angle glaucoma, in first-degree relatives, in high myopes, in diabetics, and in children. Onset is usually 2 to 6 weeks but can be days with potent agents. Check pressure at every steroid visit.
  2. Posterior subcapsular cataract, dose- and duration-dependent, and not reversible.
  3. Infection: potentiation of herpes simplex epithelial keratitis (a contraindication without antiviral cover), fungal keratitis, and Acanthamoeba; delayed epithelial and stromal healing with increased melting risk.
  4. Systemic effects even from topical or periocular use in children: adrenal suppression and growth retardation with prolonged high-dose therapy.

Always taper after more than about two weeks of use, and never stop potent steroid abruptly in uveitis: rebound inflammation is common.

Pregnancy and paediatric prescribing
  • The FDA letter categories have been replaced by narrative labelling, but the practical rules persist: prefer topical over systemic, use punctal occlusion, and choose the oldest well-characterised agent.
  • Avoid in pregnancy: tetracyclines, fluoroquinolones where alternatives exist, sulfonamides near term, carbonic anhydrase inhibitors (teratogenic in animals), and brimonidine near delivery. Erythromycin, azithromycin, and β-lactams are the safer antibacterials.
  • Prostaglandin analogues are theoretically capable of inducing uterine contraction and are usually avoided; β-blockers are commonly used with punctal occlusion but cross the placenta and enter breast milk.
  • Paediatrics: brimonidine is contraindicated under age 2 and avoided in young children (apnoea and CNS depression). Use cyclopentolate 0.5% in infants, avoid phenylephrine 10%, and remember that dose per kilogram from a standard drop is far higher in a neonate than in an adult.

  2. Why does a topical β-blocker cause bronchospasm, and how do you reduce that risk?
  3. Which glaucoma drug raises trabecular outflow, and which lowers episcleral venous pressure?
  4. Give three absolute or near-absolute cautions for oral acetazolamide.
  5. Why is brimonidine contraindicated in infants?
  6. State the sequence of management for acute angle closure and explain when pilocarpine is added.
  7. Explain the mechanism of steroid anti-inflammatory action and why NSAIDs are narrower.
  8. Which topical steroid has the lowest IOP risk and why?
  9. Prednisolone acetate versus phosphate: which penetrates an intact cornea, and why?
  10. Which antibiotic class inhibits matrix metalloproteinases, and name three ocular uses of that property.

Key
  1. Sphincter: M3; agonist pilocarpine or carbachol; antagonist atropine, cyclopentolate, tropicamide. Dilator: α1; agonist phenylephrine; antagonist dapiprazole or thymoxamine (and systemically tamsulosin).
  2. Nasolacrimal drainage delivers drug to highly vascular nasal mucosa, bypassing hepatic first-pass metabolism. Reduce it with one drop only, punctal occlusion or gentle lid closure for 2 to 3 minutes, the lowest concentration, and a β1-selective agent such as betaxolol (or avoid the class entirely).
  3. Pilocarpine (and laser trabeculoplasty and netarsudil) raise trabecular outflow; netarsudil lowers episcleral venous pressure.
  4. Sickle cell disease (acidosis promotes sickling), significant renal or hepatic failure, and true sulfonamide allergy; also avoid with high-dose salicylates.
  5. It crosses an immature blood-brain barrier and causes apnoea, bradycardia, hypotension, hypothermia, and coma; contraindicated under age 2.
  6. Topical β-blocker, α2-agonist, and CAI, plus oral or IV acetazolamide and topical steroid; corneal indentation and supine positioning; hyperosmotic if needed; then laser iridotomy in both eyes. Pilocarpine only after IOP falls below roughly 40 to 50 mmHg, because an ischaemic sphincter will not respond and it can worsen pupillary block by moving the lens forward.
  7. Steroids induce lipocortin, which inhibits phospholipase A2, blocking arachidonic acid release and therefore both prostaglandin and leukotriene production. NSAIDs inhibit only cyclooxygenase, leaving leukotrienes intact.
  8. Loteprednol etabonate, because it is an ester steroid that is rapidly hydrolysed to inactive metabolites within the eye.
  9. The acetate, because it is lipophilic and can cross the lipophilic epithelium; the hydrophilic phosphate penetrates well only through an epithelial defect.
  10. Tetracyclines, particularly doxycycline: meibomian gland dysfunction and ocular rosacea, recurrent corneal erosion, and corneal melting or peripheral ulcerative keratitis.
  11. Ciliochoroidal effusion with forward rotation of the ciliary body and anterior lens displacement, producing secondary angle closure and a myopic shift. There is no pupillary block, so an iridotomy does not address the mechanism; stop the drug and give cycloplegia, aqueous suppressants, and steroid.
  12. Above 5.0 mg/kg/day of real body weight; baseline within a year then annual screening after five years; automated visual fields and SD-OCT (adding multifocal ERG and fundus autofluorescence where available).
  13. Vigabatrin; documented baseline visual field testing and informed consent, with periodic monitoring, because the constriction is irreversible.
  14. Digoxin, causing xanthopsia; check the serum digoxin level and renal function, and consider toxicity.
  15. Pyrimethamine inhibits dihydrofolate reductase and causes bone marrow suppression; folinic acid (leucovorin) rescues host haematopoiesis without rescuing the parasite.
  16. Natamycin 5%; the Mycotic Ulcer Treatment Trial showed better outcomes than voriconazole, particularly for Fusarium.
Chapter recap
  1. Less than 5% of a drop gets in; the rest drains to nasal mucosa and bypasses first-pass metabolism.
  2. Build the autonomic table once and every mydriatic, miotic, and glaucoma item becomes derivable.
  3. Tropicamide dilates but barely cyclopleges; cyclopentolate is the refraction agent; atropine lasts two weeks.
  4. Prostaglandins raise U by 25 to 33%; β-blockers and α2-agonists and CAIs lower F; pilocarpine and netarsudil raise C.
  5. Acute angle closure: suppress, steroid, indent, position, then pilocarpine, then iridotomy both eyes.
  6. Steroids block phospholipase A2, so both prostaglandins and leukotrienes; watch pressure, cataract, infection, and healing.
  7. Loteprednol and fluorometholone for pressure-sensitive eyes; prednisolone acetate must be shaken.
  8. Fourth-generation fluoroquinolone for keratitis; ceftriaxone for gonorrhoea; azithromycin 1 g for chlamydia; natamycin for Fusarium; biguanide plus diamidine for Acanthamoeba.
  9. Doxycycline for MMP inhibition, not just for bacteria.
  10. Learn the systemic toxicity table as drug plus finding plus monitoring; hydroxychloroquine, ethambutol, vigabatrin, and topiramate are the four that carry formal protocols.
