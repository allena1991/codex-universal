The watershed at the outer plexiform layer is the highest-yield single fact here

The retinal circulation (central retinal artery) supplies the inner retina down to and including the outer plexiform layer. The choriocapillaris supplies the outer nuclear layer and the photoreceptors by diffusion across Bruch membrane and the RPE.

Therefore: a central retinal artery occlusion infarcts the inner retina, which becomes opaque and white, while the intact choroid still perfuses the foveola where the retina is thinnest, producing the cherry red spot. A choroidal occlusion instead damages photoreceptors and RPE. Every ischaemia question in retina descends from this one boundary.

Ganglion cell classes: the three parallel channels
ClassShareProjection and properties
Midget (P cells)~80% → parvocellular LGN layers 3, 4, 5, 6. Small receptive fields, sustained response, red-green opponency, high spatial and low temporal resolution. Carry central acuity and colour. In the foveola the ratio approaches one cone : one midget bipolar : one midget ganglion cell: the anatomic basis of 20/20 or better.
Parasol (M cells)~10% → magnocellular LGN layers 1, 2. Large receptive fields, transient response, high contrast sensitivity at low contrast, motion and flicker. Preferentially damaged early in glaucoma, which is the rationale for frequency-doubling and motion-based perimetry.

Intrinsically photosensitive (ipRGC)~1–3% Contain melanopsin, peak sensitivity ~480 nm. Project to the suprachiasmatic nucleus (circadian entrainment) and olivary pretectal nucleus (pupil light reflex). They explain why the pupil still reacts in some cases of profound photoreceptor loss.

Why the fovea sees best
  1. Highest cone density, with the thinnest cones and the smallest inter-receptor spacing (~2–2.5 µm), setting the Nyquist limit near 60 cycles/degree.
  2. Near one-to-one cone-to-ganglion-cell wiring: minimal spatial summation.
  3. Inner retinal layers and vessels are displaced aside so light reaches cones with minimal scatter.
  4. Cones are optically funnelled by the Stiles-Crawford effect, favouring rays through the pupil centre.

Every one of those advantages is also a vulnerability: the fovea has no capillaries, so it depends entirely on the choriocapillaris and on the perifoveal capillary ring.

The optic disc and the nerve fibre layer

  • Optic disc: 1.5 mm horizontally by 1.75–1.9 mm vertically (vertically oval), carrying ~1.2 million axons. Located ~4 mm nasal to and slightly above the fovea, corresponding to a blind spot ~15° temporal to fixation in the visual field.
  • Nerve fibre layer arrangement:
    • Papillomacular bundle: fibres from the macula enter the temporal disc directly. Selective loss gives a central or centrocaecal scotoma: toxic, nutritional, and hereditary optic neuropathies.
    • Arcuate (arciform) bundles: temporal retinal fibres arch above and below the papillomacular bundle to enter the superior and inferior poles. They respect the horizontal temporal raphe and never cross it.
    • Nasal fibres enter radially and directly.
Anatomy of the arcuate defect

Because arcuate bundles arch around the macula and stop at the horizontal raphe, damage at the superior or inferior disc pole (the thinnest neuroretinal rim, hence the ISNT rule: rim is normally thickest Inferior > Superior > Nasal > Temporal) produces a field defect that:

  • arches from the blind spot toward fixation,
  • ends abruptly at the horizontal midline nasally (a nasal step), because fibres do not cross the raphe,
  • and spares central acuity until late.

That is the glaucomatous field defect, and it is a purely anatomic prediction. A defect that crosses the horizontal midline is not arcuate and points elsewhere.

The retinal pigment epithelium

  • A single layer of ~3.5 million hexagonal cells extending from the disc to the ora serrata, where it continues as the pigmented ciliary epithelium. Cells are tall, narrow, and densely pigmented centrally (~14 µm tall, 10–14 µm wide) and flatter and broader peripherally.
  • Photoreceptor-to-RPE ratio: about 30:1 at the macula, 20:1 peripherally. Each RPE cell phagocytoses roughly that many outer segment tips daily.
  • Apical microvilli interdigitate with photoreceptor outer segments; there is no anatomic attachment, only apposition maintained by the RPE pump, the interphotoreceptor matrix, and vitreous pressure. That is why the retina detaches at this plane and why subretinal fluid resolves when RPE function returns.
  • Basal infoldings sit on Bruch membrane; the RPE basal lamina is Bruch's innermost layer.
  • Tight junctions (zonulae occludentes) between RPE cells form the outer blood-retinal barrier.

Two easily reversed facts

Rod discs are free-floating; cone discs are continuous with the membrane. That is why rods can renew an entire outer segment in ten days and why rod-specific dystrophies present with nyctalopia first.

Blue (S) cones are the rarest, comprise only about 5–10% of cones, and are absent from the very centre of the foveola (the foveal tritanopia). Nonetheless the S-cone pathway is the most vulnerable to early acquired disease, which is why acquired colour defects are usually blue-yellow and inherited defects are usually red-green (Köllner rule, with its well-known exceptions).

Retinal circulation

  • The central retinal artery, the first branch of the ophthalmic artery, enters the optic nerve ~10–12 mm behind the globe and divides into superior and inferior branches, then nasal and temporal: four arcades. Branches are end arteries with no anastomoses, which is why occlusion produces a sharply defined wedge of infarction.
  • Capillary beds: a superficial plexus in the NFL/GCL, a deep plexus at the INL/OPL borders, an intermediate plexus, and the radial peripapillary capillaries in the NFL near the disc, which are the ones whose infarction produces a cotton wool spot.
  • Inner blood-retinal barrier: non-fenestrated endothelium with tight junctions, supported by pericytes at roughly a 1:1 endothelium-to-pericyte ratio (the highest in the body). Pericyte loss is the earliest histologic lesion of diabetic retinopathy, and it precedes microaneurysm formation.
  • Cilioretinal artery: present in 15–32% of eyes, arising from the choroidal (short posterior ciliary) circulation. It spares a wedge of retina, often including the fovea, in CRAO, and conversely it is the vessel that infarcts in isolation with giant cell arteritis.
  • Retinal vessels are autoregulated and have no autonomic innervation; the choroid is not autoregulated and is richly innervated. Remember this pair.
  • Arteriole-to-venule diameter ratio is normally 2:3. Arterioles and venules share a common adventitial sheath at crossings, which is the anatomic reason hypertensive arteriolar thickening causes venous nicking and branch retinal vein occlusion at arteriovenous crossings.

The peripheral retina

  • Ora serrata: ~2 mm wide, located 6–7 mm behind the limbus nasally and 7–8 mm temporally. Dentate processes are more pronounced nasally; oral bays lie between them. Retinal thickness here falls to ~0.1 mm.
  • Long ciliary nerves run in the horizontal meridians at 3 and 9 o'clock and are the landmark separating superior from inferior on scleral depression.
  • Normal peripheral variants to distinguish from pathology: pavingstone (cobblestone) degeneration, peripheral cystoid degeneration (universal after age 8, begins at the ora temporally), honeycomb (reticular) degeneration, pars plana cysts, granular tissue and meridional folds.
  • Lattice degeneration: present in 6–10% of the population, bilateral in ~50%, most often in the vertical meridians. Histologically shows inner retinal thinning, overlying vitreous liquefaction, and firm vitreous adhesion at its margins with sclerotic vessels. That combination is why lattice causes horseshoe tears at its posterior edge and atrophic holes within it.

Key
  1. ILM, NFL, GCL, IPL, INL, OPL, ONL, ELM, photoreceptor layer, RPE.
  2. The outer plexiform layer; the inner retina whitens while the choroid still perfuses the thin foveola, giving a cherry red spot.
  3. Inner: retinal capillary endothelium with tight junctions and pericytes. Outer: RPE tight junctions.
  4. Pericyte loss, preceding microaneurysms and basement membrane thickening.
  5. Arcuate nerve fibre bundles arch around the macula and do not cross the horizontal temporal raphe.
  6. Neuroretinal rim thickness normally Inferior > Superior > Nasal > Temporal; violation suggests glaucomatous rim loss.
  7. Parasol / magnocellular cells; frequency-doubling technology perimetry (and motion-based tests).
  8. Intrinsically photosensitive retinal ganglion cells; to the suprachiasmatic nucleus and the olivary pretectal nucleus.
  9. Lutein and zeaxanthin, concentrated in the Henle fibre layer of the macula.
  10. Highest cone density with smallest spacing, near one-to-one cone-to-ganglion-cell wiring, and lateral displacement of inner layers and vessels.
  11. Rod discs are detached from the plasma membrane and are fully renewed in about 10 days; cone discs are continuous with it. Rod turnover dependence makes rods fail first in RPE phagocytic disease, giving nyctalopia.
  12. Retinal vessels are autoregulated with no autonomic supply; choroidal vessels are richly innervated and not autoregulated.
  13. The vitreous is liquefied over the lesion but firmly adherent at its margins, so traction concentrates at the posterior edge.
Chapter recap
  1. Ten layers, two limiting membranes both made by Müller cells, and one watershed at the OPL.
  2. Retinal circulation inner, choroid outer; two barriers, endothelial tight junctions and RPE tight junctions.
  3. Macula 5.5 mm, fovea 1.5 mm, foveola 0.35 mm and rod-free, FAZ 0.4 to 0.5 mm.
  4. 1.2 million ganglion cells in four classes: midget/parvo, parasol/magno, bistratified/konio, and melanopsin ipRGC.
  5. The arcuate bundle and the horizontal raphe explain the glaucomatous field; the papillomacular bundle explains toxic central scotomas.
  6. RPE does six jobs; each failure has a named disease.
  7. Rods: 120 million, rhodopsin at 500 nm, free discs, slow dark adaptation. Cones: 6 million, three opsins, membrane-continuous discs.
  8. Pericyte loss starts diabetic retinopathy; the 1:1 pericyte ratio is the highest in the body.
  9. Retinal arteries are end arteries; a cilioretinal artery in up to a third of eyes changes the CRAO picture.
  10. Lattice: 6 to 10% of people, liquefied centre, adherent margins, tears posteriorly.
