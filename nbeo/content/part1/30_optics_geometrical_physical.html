Abbe number, and the polycarbonate trade-off

Crown glass V ≈ 59; CR-39 V ≈ 58; Trivex V ≈ 43–45; polycarbonate V ≈ 30; 1.67 high index V ≈ 32; 1.74 V ≈ 33.

Polycarbonate has the best impact resistance and the worst chromatic dispersion of the common materials. That is the trade-off in a high-prescription patient who notices colour fringing at the lens periphery: Trivex gives comparable impact safety with a better Abbe number and lower specific gravity, at slightly greater thickness. Polycarbonate remains mandatory in monocular patients, children, and sports eyewear.

  • The circle of least confusion sits at the dioptric midpoint of the two focal lines, which is why the spherical equivalent = sphere + cyl/2.
  • With-the-rule: steeper (more powerful) meridian is vertical, so minus cylinder axis is near 180°. Typical in youth. Against-the-rule: minus cyl axis near 90°, typical with age and after superior-incision cataract surgery. Oblique: axis between 30–60° or 120–150°.
  • Regular astigmatism has two perpendicular principal meridians and is correctable with a cylinder. Irregular astigmatism does not, and requires a rigid contact lens or wavefront-guided correction.
  • Lenticular versus corneal: compare refractive cylinder with keratometric cylinder. Residual (internal) astigmatism averages about 0.50 D against the rule, the basis of the Javal rule: total astigmatism ≈ 1.25 × corneal astigmatism − 0.50 × 90.
Transposition, in three steps
  1. Add the sphere and the cylinder algebraically to get the new sphere.
  2. Change the sign of the cylinder.
  3. Rotate the axis by 90° (add 90 if the axis is 90 or less, subtract 90 if greater).

Worked example. −2.00 −1.50 × 180 becomes −3.50 +1.50 × 090. And +1.25 +0.75 × 135 becomes +2.00 −0.75 × 045. Check by confirming the two principal meridian powers are unchanged: in the first example the 180 meridian is −2.00 and the 090 meridian is −3.50 in both forms.

Aberrations

Monochromatic and chromatic aberrations
AberrationMechanismOphthalmic manifestation and management
Spherical aberrationPeripheral rays refract more strongly than paraxial rays Night myopia and halos with a large pupil; reduced by an aspheric lens surface, a smaller pupil, or aspheric IOL design. The normal cornea has positive spherical aberration partly offset by the negative aberration of the young lens.
ComaOff-axis point images become comet-shaped; asymmetric aberration Prominent in keratoconus and after decentred refractive surgery; a leading cause of monocular ghosting that spectacles cannot correct.
Oblique (radial or marginal) astigmatismOff-axis rays meet a tilted surface Blur in the spectacle periphery; controlled by corrected-curve (best-form) lens design using Tscherning ellipse base curves.
Curvature of fieldImage surface is curved, not flatAlso managed by best-form design.
DistortionMagnification varies with field angle Pincushion in plus (aphakic) lenses and barrel in minus lenses. Aphakic spectacles also produce the ring scotoma (jack-in-the-box phenomenon) from prismatic effect at the lens edge.
Longitudinal (axial) chromatic aberrationDispersion: short wavelengths refract more The eye has about 2 D of chromatic aberration between the ends of the visible spectrum. This is the basis of the duochrome (bichrome) test: with red and green targets, a myope (or over-minused patient) sees green clearer and a hyperope sees red clearer. "RAM-GAP": Red Add Minus, Green Add Plus.
Transverse (lateral) chromatic aberrationDispersion off-axis, producing coloured fringes Worse with low Abbe number and with looking away from the optical centre; the practical complaint in polycarbonate.
Higher-order aberrations (Zernike)Trefoil, quadrafoil, secondary astigmatism, spherical aberration terms Quantified by wavefront aberrometry as RMS error; the target of wavefront-guided ablation and custom lenses. Only about 10–15% of ocular blur in a typical eye is higher order.
DiffractionFundamental wave limit at any aperture Sets the theoretical acuity ceiling. Combined with aberrations, optimum optical performance occurs at a pupil of about 2–3 mm: smaller pupils lose to diffraction, larger pupils lose to aberration.

Why the far point concept solves refraction problems

The far point is the object point conjugate to the retina with accommodation relaxed. A correcting lens must place its secondary focal point at the eye's far point. For a −5.00 D myope, the far point is 20 cm in front of the eye; for a +5.00 D hyperope it is 20 cm behind the eye (virtual). Every vertex distance, effective power, and contact-lens-versus-spectacle question is answered by asking where the far point is and where the lens's focal point falls.

Physical optics

Wave properties

  • Speed of light in vacuum 3 × 108 m/s; in a medium, v = c/n. Frequency does not change with medium; wavelength does.
  • Visible spectrum 380–760 nm. Violet-blue short, red long. Photon energy E = hν = hc/λ, so shorter wavelengths carry more energy: the reason UV is phototoxic and infrared is thermally damaging.
  • Interference: coherent waves add. Anti-reflection coating requires a layer of optical thickness λ/4 and refractive index equal to √nlens so the two reflections cancel. High index materials reflect more light (about 4% per surface at n = 1.5, more at 1.74) so AR coating benefits them most.
  • Diffraction: an aperture spreads light into an Airy disc. Resolution θ = 1.22λ/d. With λ = 555 nm and d = 3 mm, θ ≈ 2.3 × 10−4 radians ≈ 0.78 arcmin, slightly better than 20/20. Diffractive multifocal IOLs use concentric steps to split light between two orders.
  • Polarisation: unpolarised light becomes linearly polarised by reflection at the Brewster angle (tanθB = n′/n; about 53° for air to glass). Because glare reflected from horizontal surfaces is horizontally polarised, sunglasses use a vertical transmission axis. Crossed polarisers are the basis of the Titmus and Randot stereotests, vectographic acuity charts, and of the Haidinger brush entoptic phenomenon used to test macular fixation.
  • Scatter: Rayleigh scatter varies as 1/λ4 (blue sky, blue irides with little stromal melanin, and the faint blue of a normal cornea). Mie scatter occurs from particles comparable to the wavelength and is relatively wavelength-independent (the white of a cataract or a scarred cornea). The Tyndall effect is the visualisation of scatter by a narrow beam, which is exactly how the slit lamp reveals aqueous cells and flare.

Laser light has three defining properties: monochromaticity, coherence (temporal and spatial), and collimation. Production requires population inversion in a gain medium, pumped by an external energy source, within an optical cavity. Energy delivered depends on power, spot size, and duration; irradiance is inversely proportional to the square of the spot diameter, which is why halving spot size quadruples the effect at the same power.

Fluorescence and imaging optics

Diagnostic imaging physics
ModalityPhysics and key numbers
Fluorescein angiography Sodium fluorescein absorbs blue at 465–490 nm and emits green at 520–530 nm; an exciter filter and a barrier filter isolate the emission. About 70–80% is protein-bound, so the free fraction leaks from incompetent vessels. Phases: choroidal flush at ~10–12 s, arterial, arteriovenous, venous, then recirculation and late phase. Fluoresces yellow-green through a cobalt filter at the slit lamp, which is the same physics used for tear break-up and Seidel testing.
Indocyanine green angiography Absorbs ~805 nm, emits ~835 nm (infrared). Because it is 98% protein bound and infrared penetrates blood, exudate, and pigment, it images the choroidal circulation: polypoidal vasculopathy, occult CNV, central serous chorioretinopathy, and white dot syndromes. Contains iodine: contraindicated in iodine or shellfish allergy and in significant hepatic disease.
Optical coherence tomography Low-coherence interferometry using near-infrared light around 840 nm (or 1050 nm for swept-source, giving deeper choroidal penetration). Axial resolution ~5 µm, determined by the light source bandwidth, not the pupil. Spectral-domain uses a spectrometer and a fixed reference arm; swept-source uses a tunable laser. OCT angiography detects motion contrast from flowing erythrocytes and requires no dye.
Ultrasound Sound velocity 1532 m/s in aqueous and vitreous, 1641 m/s in the lens, ~1550 m/s average for a phakic eye. A-scan (8–10 MHz) for biometry and tissue reflectivity; B-scan (10 MHz) for two-dimensional structure; ultrasound biomicroscopy (50–80 MHz) for angle and ciliary body at the cost of penetration. Higher frequency means better resolution and less penetration, always. Immersion A-scan avoids the corneal compression error of contact technique.
Optical biometry (IOLMaster, Lenstar) Partial coherence interferometry or swept-source; measures to the RPE rather than the internal limiting membrane, so values are calibrated to be comparable to immersion ultrasound. More reproducible, and it fails in dense cataract or macular pathology preventing fixation.
Corneal topography and tomography Placido disc reflection maps the anterior surface only. Scheimpflug photography and OCT-based tomography image both surfaces plus pachymetry, which is essential for detecting posterior elevation in early ectasia.

Ophthalmic instrument optics

How each instrument works optically
InstrumentOptical principle
Direct ophthalmoscopeForms an erect, virtual, ~15× magnified image; field of view only about 7–10°; magnification varies with the patient's refractive error (larger in myopia, smaller in hyperopia).
Binocular indirect ophthalmoscopy The condensing lens forms a real, inverted, laterally reversed aerial image. Magnification ≈ 60 / Flens: a 20 D lens gives about 3× with a wide field; a 30 D lens gives about 2× with a wider field and shorter working distance. Higher lens power means lower magnification and a larger field.
Fundus biomicroscopy lenses Non-contact 78 D and 90 D lenses give an inverted, reversed image; the 78 D gives more magnification, the 90 D a wider field. Contact lenses (Goldmann three-mirror, Hruby) neutralise corneal power.
Slit lampAn illumination system in Koehler configuration plus a Galilean telescope in each ocular, with a convergent objective; magnification typically 6–40×. The illumination and observation systems are coupled to a common pivot.
Gonioscopy lens Overcomes the critical angle at the cornea-air interface (~46°). Indirect lenses (Goldmann, Zeiss four-mirror, Posner, Sussman) use a mirror so you view the opposite quadrant; the direct Koeppe lens gives an upright view.
Keratometer Treats the cornea as a convex mirror and measures the size of the reflected image of a target of known size at a known distance, converting radius to power with the fictitious index 1.3375. Measures a paracentral ~3 mm zone, which is why it misses central irregularity.
Retinoscope A streak (or spot) projected into the eye; the reflex from the retina is observed. With motion means the far point is behind the peephole (need plus); against motion means it is between the eye and the peephole (need minus). At neutrality the far point coincides with the peephole, so you subtract the working distance dioptric value (1.50 D at 67 cm, 2.00 D at 50 cm).
Lensmeter (focimeter)Measures back vertex power by a Badal optometer arrangement; the target moves until the emerging bundle is collimated.
Applanation tonometerTwo prisms split the fluorescein ring into semicircles displaced by exactly half the ring diameter, so the inner edges touch when the applanated area equals 7.35 mm².
Specular microscope / pachymeterSpecular reflection from the endothelium-aqueous interface; optical pachymetry uses the split-image doubling principle, ultrasonic uses time-of-flight.

  2. A −9.00 D spectacle lens is worn at a 14 mm vertex distance. What contact lens power is equivalent?
  3. A patient wears +6.00 D and is decentred 4 mm nasally from the optical centre in each eye. How much prism is induced, and in which direction?
  4. Transpose −3.25 +2.00 × 065.
  5. What is the power of −1.00 −3.00 × 180 in the 60° meridian?
  6. Give the spherical equivalent of +2.50 −1.50 × 090.
  7. A biconvex lens has surface powers of +10.00 D and +6.00 D, centre thickness 6 mm, index 1.5. What is the equivalent power?
  8. Compute the critical angle for the cornea (n = 1.376) to air.
  9. Using the Rayleigh criterion at 555 nm, what pupil size gives 1 arcmin of resolution?
  10. A patient reports the red letters are clearer on duochrome. What is the refractive status of the current lens, and what do you do?
  11. What is the magnification of a +16.00 D hand magnifier in conventional terms?
  12. A Galilean telescope has a +30 D objective and a −90 D eyepiece. What is its magnification and image orientation?
Key
  1. L = 1/(−0.25) = −4.00 D; L′ = −4 + 8 = +4.00 D, so l′ = +25 cm (real, right of lens). m = L/L′ = −4/+4 = −1.00, i.e. same size, inverted.
  2. Fnew = F / (1 − dF) = −9 / (1 − (0.014)(−9)) = −9 / 1.126 = −7.99, so −8.00 D. Moving a minus lens closer requires less minus.
  3. Δ = 0.4 cm × 6.00 D = 2.4 Δ per eye. Decentring a plus lens nasally moves the optical centre nasally, giving base-in prism in each eye, so 4.8 Δ base-in total.
  4. −1.25 −2.00 × 155.
  5. θ is measured from the cylinder axis (180), so θ = 60°. F = −1.00 + (−3.00)(sin260) = −1.00 + (−3.00)(0.75) = −3.25 D.
  6. +2.50 + (−1.50/2) = +1.75 D.
  7. Fe = 10 + 6 − (0.006/1.5)(10)(6) = 16 − (0.004)(60) = 16 − 0.24 = +15.76 D.
  8. sinθc = 1.000/1.376 = 0.7267, θc = 46.6°.
  9. 1 arcmin = 2.909 × 10−4 rad. d = 1.22λ/θ = 1.22(555 × 10−9)/2.909 × 10−4 = 2.33 mm.
  10. Red clearer means the eye is effectively under-minused or over-plussed: the current lens leaves the patient myopic relative to the target. Add minus (RAM: Red Add Minus) until they equalise or green is just barely clearer.
  11. M = F/4 = 16/4 = 4×.
  12. M = −Fe/Fo = −(−90)/(+30) = 3×, and the image is upright (a Galilean telescope gives an erect image).
Chapter recap
  1. L′ = L + F solves most problems; F = (n′ − n)/r for surfaces; Fe = F1 + F2 − (t/n)F1F2 for thick lenses.
  2. Prentice: Δ = c(cm) × F; 1 Δ = 0.57°; light bends to the base, images shift to the apex.
  3. Transposition: add algebraically, flip the sign, rotate 90°. Spherical equivalent is sphere plus half the cylinder.
  4. Abbe number is dispersion resistance: polycarbonate is the toughest and the most dispersive.
  5. Aberrations: spherical for halos, coma for keratoconus, oblique astigmatism for best-form design, chromatic for duochrome (RAM-GAP).
  6. Reduced eye: +60 D, n = 1.336, nodal point 17 mm from retina, 0.29 mm per degree, 1 mm equals 3 D.
  7. Rayleigh 1.22λ/d gives 1 arcmin at about 2.3 mm; optimum pupil is 2 to 3 mm.
  8. Rayleigh scatter for blue, Mie for white, Tyndall for the slit lamp beam.
  9. Laser interactions: photocoagulation thermal, photodisruption at 1064 nm, photoablation at 193 nm, photochemical at 689 nm.
  10. BIO magnification ≈ 60/F: higher lens power gives less magnification and more field.
