The iris

Surface anatomy

  • Diameter ~12 mm; thickest at the collarette (~0.6 mm), thinnest at the root (~0.1 mm), which is why the root is where iridodialysis and peripheral iridotomy occur.
  • The collarette divides the pupillary zone (inner third, containing the sphincter) from the ciliary zone (outer two thirds, containing the dilator). It marks where the embryonic pupillary membrane attached; persistent pupillary membrane strands arise from it.
  • Pupil diameter ranges 1–8 mm, decentred slightly nasally and inferiorly. Physiologic anisocoria of up to 0.4–1.0 mm occurs in about 20% of people and is equal in light and dark.
  • Crypts of Fuchs: stromal openings near the collarette and root. Contraction furrows: circumferential grooves in the ciliary zone. Fuchs freckles (naevi) sit on the anterior border layer; Brushfield spots (Down syndrome) are areas of relative stromal hyperplasia; Lisch nodules (neurofibromatosis 1) are melanocytic hamartomas of the anterior border layer.

Layers, anterior to posterior

Iris layers and their embryonic origin
LayerOriginComposition and clinical relevance
Anterior border layerNeural crest mesenchyme Condensed stroma of fibroblasts and melanocytes. There is no endothelium and no epithelium on the anterior iris surface, so the stroma is in direct contact with aqueous. Naevi and Lisch nodules sit here. Loss of this layer in pigment dispersion gives the "moth-eaten" transillumination pattern.
StromaNeural crest mesenchyme Loose collagen, melanocytes, mast cells, radially oriented vessels with characteristically thick collagenous walls, nerves, and the sphincter pupillae. Iris colour is determined by the amount of melanin in these stromal melanocytes, not by their number.
Anterior (myo)epitheliumNeuroectoderm, outer layer of optic cup Pigmented cuboidal cells whose basal processes form the dilator pupillae: this is a myoepithelium, an unusual arrangement worth remembering.
Posterior pigment epitheliumNeuroectoderm, inner layer of optic cup Densely pigmented columnar cells, continuous with the non-pigmented ciliary epithelium and, embryologically, with the neurosensory retina. It curls around the pupil margin to form the pupillary ruff. Its pigment is what disperses in pigmentary glaucoma and what forms Krukenberg spindle.

Iris vasculature

  • The major arterial circle lies in the ciliary body, not the iris, and is formed by the two long posterior ciliary arteries and the anterior ciliary arteries. Radial iris arterioles run from it to a minor arterial circle near the collarette.
  • Iris capillaries are non-fenestrated with tight junctions: together with the non-pigmented ciliary epithelium they constitute the blood-aqueous barrier. Breakdown of this barrier is what produces cells and flare.
  • Normal iris vessels are radial and regular. Neovascularisation of the iris is irregular, arborising, and begins at the pupil margin and in the angle: that distinction is the observation on which neovascular glaucoma is diagnosed.

  • Total width ~6 mm from the scleral spur to the ora serrata. Triangular in cross section with the base facing forward.
  • Pars plicata (corona ciliaris), anterior ~2 mm, carrying 70–80 ciliary processes. This is the site of aqueous formation and of zonular origin.
  • Pars plana, posterior ~4 mm, smooth and relatively avascular. It is wider temporally than nasally, which is why the standard vitrectomy sclerotomy is placed 3.5–4.0 mm posterior to the limbus in a phakic adult (3.0–3.5 mm in a pseudophake, less in a child).

Layers, outside to inside

  1. Supraciliary lamina (continuous with the lamina fusca and the suprachoroidal space). Fluid accumulation here is a ciliochoroidal detachment; it is also the potential space that cyclodialysis exploits.
  2. Ciliary muscle (see below).
  3. Stroma with the major arterial circle and vessels of the ciliary processes.
  4. Pigmented ciliary epithelium: continuous with the retinal pigment epithelium.
  5. Non-pigmented ciliary epithelium: continuous with the neurosensory retina. Tight junctions between non-pigmented cells form the blood-aqueous barrier, and these cells are rich in Na+/K+ ATPase and carbonic anhydrase II: the two pharmacologic targets for suppressing aqueous production.
  6. Internal limiting membrane, continuous with the retinal ILM.
Ciliary muscle: three parts, two consequences
PortionOrientation and insertionFunctional effect
Longitudinal (Brücke)Outermost, meridional, inserts into the scleral spur and trabecular meshwork Contraction puts traction on the meshwork, widening its spaces and increasing trabecular outflow. This is precisely how pilocarpine lowers IOP, and it simultaneously reduces uveoscleral outflow.
Radial / obliqueMiddle, fan-shapedIntermediate.
Circular (Müller)Innermost, sphincter-like, near the processes Contraction moves the ciliary body forward and inward, slackening the zonules and allowing the lens to become more convex: accommodation.

Innervation: parasympathetic from CN III via the ciliary ganglion and short ciliary nerves acting on M3 receptors to contract; sympathetic β2 input relaxes it (a minor effect clinically, but the reason β-blockers can slightly shift accommodation).

The choroid

  • Thickness 0.22–0.25 mm at the posterior pole, thinning to ~0.1 mm anteriorly. Extends from the optic nerve to the ora serrata.
  • Layers, outer to inner: lamina fusca / suprachoroid → Haller layer (large vessels) → Sattler layer (medium vessels) → choriocapillaris → Bruch membrane.
  • The choriocapillaris is fenestrated and has the highest blood flow per unit weight of any tissue in the body, with a low arteriovenous oxygen difference. Its jobs are to nourish the outer retina across Bruch membrane and to act as a heat sink for absorbed light.
  • Bruch membrane, five layers: RPE basal lamina, inner collagenous zone, elastic layer, outer collagenous zone, choriocapillaris endothelial basal lamina. Thickens and calcifies with age; breaks here permit choroidal neovascularisation and produce lacquer cracks in pathologic myopia and angioid streaks in pseudoxanthoma elasticum, Paget disease, sickle cell disease, and Ehlers-Danlos.
  • Supply: short posterior ciliary arteries feed the choriocapillaris in a lobular, segmental pattern (hence triangular Amalric infarcts and Elschnig spots); long posterior ciliary arteries and anterior ciliary arteries feed the anterior choroid. Drainage is by the vortex veins. The watershed zone near the disc explains the pattern of ischaemic optic neuropathy.
  • Innervation by long and short ciliary nerves; the choroid is richly supplied with sympathetic vasoconstrictor and parasympathetic vasodilator fibres.

Gonioscopic anatomy

Angle structures, anterior to posterior

"I Can See Some Structures" read backwards from the cornea inward, or learn the forward sequence directly:

Schwalbe line → Trabecular meshwork → Scleral spur → Ciliary body band → Iris root

Forward mnemonic: "Steep TRAil Sends Cattle Inward", or simply remember the white line, the meshwork, the white spur, the brown band, the iris.

What each angle landmark looks like and means
StructureGonioscopic appearanceSignificance
Schwalbe lineFine translucent ridge; the termination of Descemet membrane. Located by the corneal wedge: the point where the two beams of an optic section meet. A prominent, anteriorly displaced Schwalbe line is posterior embryotoxon, seen in Axenfeld-Rieger. Pigment on it is the Sampaolesi line of pigment dispersion syndrome and pseudoexfoliation.
Trabecular meshworkGround-glass band; anterior (non-filtering) portion is pale, posterior (filtering) portion overlying Schlemm canal is greyer and takes up pigment. Blood in Schlemm canal is visible here when episcleral venous pressure exceeds IOP (carotid-cavernous fistula, Sturge-Weber, or over-compression with the goniolens).
Scleral spurThe whitest, most sharply defined line in the angle; the posterior lip of the internal scleral sulcus. The universal reference landmark: the angle is called "open to the spur", "open to the ciliary body band", and so on. Insertion point of the longitudinal ciliary muscle.
Ciliary body bandGrey to dark brown band; the anterior face of the ciliary body. Abnormally wide band means angle recession from blunt trauma, a cause of late unilateral glaucoma. Also wide in myopia and aphakia.
Iris root / processesIris insertion; fine iris processes (of Busacca) may bridge to the meshwork. Distinguish delicate, lace-like iris processes from broad, tent-like peripheral anterior synechiae, which obliterate the view of underlying structures.

Trabecular meshwork microanatomy and outflow

Three trabecular layers, inner to outer
LayerPore sizeRole
Uveal meshwork25–75 µmInnermost, from iris root to Schwalbe line. Largest openings, negligible resistance.
Corneoscleral meshwork5–50 µmMiddle, sheet-like beams from scleral spur to the lateral wall of the scleral sulcus.
Juxtacanalicular (cribriform) tissuesmallest The principal site of normal aqueous outflow resistance. Ground substance plus the inner wall endothelium of Schlemm canal. This is where extracellular matrix accumulates in primary open-angle glaucoma.
  • Schlemm canal: an endothelium-lined circumferential channel, 190–370 µm in diameter, whose inner wall cells form giant vacuoles that transport aqueous transcellularly. 25–35 collector channels leave it for the deep and intrascleral venous plexuses and then the episcleral veins. Direct connections that carry clear aqueous are the aqueous veins of Ascher, visible at the slit lamp as a clear stream laminating with blood.
  • Outflow split: conventional (trabecular) route ~80–90%, pressure-dependent; uveoscleral (unconventional) route ~10–20%, largely pressure-independent, through the ciliary muscle interstitium into the supraciliary space and out through the sclera.
Two drugs, two opposite muscle effects on two different routes
  • Pilocarpine (muscarinic agonist): ciliary muscle contracts → spur traction → increases trabecular outflow, but simultaneously decreases uveoscleral outflow by compacting the muscle bundles.
  • Prostaglandin analogues: remodel ciliary muscle extracellular matrix via matrix metalloproteinases → increases uveoscleral outflow predominantly.
  • Atropine / cycloplegics: muscle relaxes → uveoscleral outflow increases slightly, trabecular outflow falls. In an anatomically narrow angle this can precipitate angle closure, which is a mechanical, not a pharmacologic, effect.

Capsule, epithelium, fibres

  • Capsule: the thickest basement membrane in the body, type IV collagen, secreted by the epithelium. Anterior capsule ~14–15.5 µm (thickest pre-equatorially), posterior capsule ~4 µm (thinnest at the posterior pole). That single asymmetry explains why posterior capsule rupture is the feared complication of phacoemulsification and why posterior lenticonus and posterior subcapsular opacity behave as they do.
  • Epithelium: a single layer beneath the anterior capsule only. There is no posterior lens epithelium after embryogenesis. The germinative (pre-equatorial) zone divides; new cells migrate to the equator, elongate, lose their organelles, and become fibres. Migration of these cells across the posterior capsule after cataract surgery causes posterior capsule opacification (and Elschnig pearls, and Soemmering ring).
  • Sutures: fibres from opposite sides meet in sutures. The fetal nucleus shows an upright Y anteriorly and an inverted Y posteriorly. The embryonic nucleus (from the primary lens fibres) has no sutures.
  • Zones, oldest to newest: embryonic nucleus → fetal nucleus → infantile nucleus → adult nucleus → cortex. The centre is the oldest tissue in the body.
  • Zonules of Zinn: fibrillin-rich fibres from the basal lamina of the non-pigmented epithelium in the valleys between ciliary processes (pars plana and plicata) to the lens capsule pre-equatorially and post-equatorially, leaving a zonule-free zone at the equator.
  • The lens is avascular and non-innervated. All nutrition and waste exchange occur through the aqueous, predominantly anteriorly.

The vitreous

  • Volume ~4.0 mL, about 80% of the globe's volume. 98–99% water, held as a gel by a scaffold of type II collagen filled with hyaluronan. Refractive index 1.336. Hyalocytes populate the cortex.
  • Named spaces and remnants:
    • Patellar fossa: the saucer-shaped depression accommodating the posterior lens.
    • Berger space: the retrolental space between posterior capsule and anterior hyaloid.
    • Wieger (hyaloideocapsular) ligament: annular attachment of the anterior hyaloid to the posterior capsule; its anterior margin is the ring of Egger.
    • Cloquet canal: the S-shaped remnant of the primary vitreous running from the posterior lens to the disc. Its funnel-shaped termination at the disc is the area of Martegiani.
    • A persistent hyaloid artery remnant on the disc is a Bergmeister papilla; on the lens it is a Mittendorf dot (typically inferonasal).
Vitreoretinal adhesion, strongest to weakest
  1. Vitreous base: straddles the ora serrata, extending ~2 mm anterior and ~3–4 mm posterior to it. Adhesion here is so strong that the base essentially never detaches; traction instead avulses retina, producing giant retinal tears and dialyses at its posterior margin.
  2. Margins of lattice degeneration and chorioretinal scars: abnormally firm focal adhesion, which is why lattice generates horseshoe tears at its posterior edge.
  3. Posterior lens capsule (Wieger ligament) in the young eye.
  4. Optic disc margin: separation releases a glial annulus, the Weiss ring, the pathognomonic sign of complete posterior vitreous detachment.
  5. Perimacular / foveal attachment: incomplete separation here causes vitreomacular traction, macular hole, and epiretinal membrane.
  6. Along major retinal vessels: traction here tears a vessel and causes vitreous haemorrhage without a retinal break.
  7. Rest of the posterior retina: weakest, so this is where separation begins.

Ageing. Hyaluronan-collagen dissociation produces syneresis (liquefaction) and syneretic cavities, then collapse and posterior vitreous detachment. PVD is the proximate cause of most acute symptomatic floaters, of most retinal tears, and of the single most important history question in an acute floaters presentation.

Key
  1. The anterior (myo)epithelium, derived from the outer layer of the optic cup; it is a myoepithelium, unusual for a muscle to arise from an epithelial layer.
  2. There is no anterior endothelium or epithelium, only a condensed anterior border layer, so drugs and pigment interact directly with stroma.
  3. The two long posterior ciliary arteries and the anterior ciliary arteries; it lies in the ciliary body, not the iris.
  4. Non-pigmented ciliary epithelium, joined by tight junctions; Na+/K+ ATPase and carbonic anhydrase II.
  5. Longitudinal (Brücke) fibres pull on the scleral spur, opening the trabecular spaces and increasing conventional outflow; uveoscleral outflow decreases.
  6. Schwalbe line, trabecular meshwork, scleral spur, ciliary body band, iris root; the scleral spur is the reference landmark.
  7. Juxtacanalicular (cribriform) tissue with the inner wall of Schlemm canal.
  8. Blunt ocular trauma with angle recession; late unilateral open-angle glaucoma.
  9. Anterior about 14 to 15.5 µm, posterior about 4 µm; the posterior capsule is fragile and is the structure at risk in phacoemulsification.
  10. Posterior epithelial cells elongate into the primary lens fibres during embryogenesis and are consumed; migration of residual equatorial epithelium onto the posterior capsule causes posterior capsule opacification.
  11. Superotemporal = Marfan, FBN1 fibrillin-1. Inferonasal = homocystinuria, cystathionine β-synthase deficiency.
  12. Vitreous base (giant retinal tear or dialysis), lattice margins (horseshoe tear), optic disc (Weiss ring with PVD).
Chapter recap
  1. Iris: four layers, two from neural crest and two from neuroectoderm; sphincter is parasympathetic via short ciliary nerves, dilator sympathetic via long ciliary nerves.
  2. Ciliary body 6 mm: pars plicata 2 mm secretes aqueous, pars plana 4 mm is the surgical entry.
  3. Brücke to the spur opens the meshwork; Müller slackens zonules for accommodation.
  4. Blood-aqueous barrier = non-pigmented ciliary epithelium tight junctions plus non-fenestrated iris capillaries.
  5. Choriocapillaris is fenestrated with the highest blood flow per gram in the body; Bruch membrane breaks give CNV, lacquer cracks, and angioid streaks.
  6. Angle: Schwalbe, meshwork, spur, band, root. Juxtacanalicular tissue is the resistor. 80 to 90% trabecular, 10 to 20% uveoscleral.
  7. Lens grows forever, has no posterior epithelium, a 4 µm posterior capsule, and a gradient index.
  8. Lens dislocation direction names the metabolic disease.
  9. Vitreous is 4 mL of 99% water on a type II collagen and hyaluronan scaffold.
  10. Adhesion order: base, lattice margins, capsule, disc, macula, vessels, posterior retina. That order predicts every traction lesion.
