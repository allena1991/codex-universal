Metabolic pathways you must be able to place

Core pathways: location, yield, and ocular relevance
PathwayLocationYield / purpose and ocular relevance
Glycolysis (Embden-Meyerhof)Cytosol Glucose → 2 pyruvate, net 2 ATP and 2 NADH anaerobically. Rate-limiting enzymes: hexokinase (glucokinase in liver) and phosphofructokinase-1. Dominant in the lens and the corneal epithelium, both of which are relatively hypoxic.
TCA (Krebs) cycle and oxidative phosphorylationMitochondria ~36–38 ATP per glucose. Requires oxygen. Dominant in the corneal endothelium, ciliary epithelium, retinal inner segments, and RPE. Lens fibres have no mitochondria, so they cannot use it.
Pentose phosphate (hexose monophosphate) shuntCytosol Generates NADPH and ribose-5-phosphate. NADPH is required to regenerate reduced glutathione and to drive aldose reductase. Unusually active in the corneal epithelium (~35% of glucose) and the lens (~5%). Rate-limiting enzyme: glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase; its deficiency causes haemolysis with sulfonamides and with some topical agents.
Polyol (sorbitol) pathwayCytosol Aldose reductase (uses NADPH) converts glucose → sorbitol; sorbitol dehydrogenase converts sorbitol → fructose. Normally minor (~5%), but aldose reductase has a high Km, so it takes over in hyperglycaemia. The pathway of diabetic cataract, and implicated in diabetic neuropathy and retinopathy.
Glycogen metabolismCytosol The corneal epithelium and Müller cells store glycogen as a local energy buffer, which is why the epithelium can survive brief hypoxia and why Müller cells sustain retinal neurons.
Glutathione cycleCytosol GSH (a tripeptide: glutamate, cysteine, glycine) is the main intracellular reducing agent. Glutathione peroxidase detoxifies H2O2; glutathione reductase regenerates GSH using NADPH. Lens GSH concentration is very high and falls with age and with cataract.

Sugar cataract in two steps, and why galactose is worse
  1. Osmotic step. Hyperglycaemia saturates hexokinase, so excess glucose enters the polyol pathway. Aldose reductase makes sorbitol, which is impermeant and cannot leave the fibre. Sorbitol dehydrogenase is comparatively slow, so sorbitol accumulates, water follows osmotically, fibres swell and rupture, and the lens opacifies. This is the mechanism of the acute, sometimes reversible, refractive shift and cataract of poorly controlled diabetes.
  2. Oxidative step. Aldose reductase consumes NADPH, competing with glutathione reductase. GSH falls, thiol groups on crystallins oxidise and cross-link, and protein aggregates scatter light irreversibly.

Galactose is worse because aldose reductase converts it to galactitol (dulcitol), and there is no dehydrogenase to metabolise galactitol further. It therefore accumulates without limit, which is why classic galactosaemia produces a rapid oil-droplet cataract in infancy.

Lens proteins

  • Protein content ~33% by weight, the highest of any tissue. Water ~66%.
  • Crystallins, about 90% of lens protein:
    • α-crystallin: the largest, and a small heat-shock protein that functions as a molecular chaperone, binding partially denatured proteins and keeping them soluble. Loss of chaperone capacity with age is a central event in cataractogenesis.
    • β-crystallin: most abundant by number, oligomeric.
    • γ-crystallin: smallest, monomeric, highest in the nucleus. Mutations cause several congenital cataracts.
  • Albuminoid is the water-insoluble fraction; it rises with age and correlates with nuclear opacity.
  • Post-translational damage that accumulates because lens proteins are never replaced: non-enzymatic glycation, deamidation, racemisation of amino acids, disulfide cross-linking, carbamylation (in uraemia, hence uraemic cataract), and photo-oxidation by UV-A. These are the molecular reasons the lens centre is the oldest and most vulnerable protein pool in the body.
  • Ion homeostasis: a pump-leak system maintains high intracellular potassium and low sodium, driven by Na+/K+ ATPase concentrated in the anterior epithelium. Pump failure produces cortical hydration and cortical cataract. This is why the anterior epithelium is described as the metabolic engine of an otherwise inert tissue.

Corneal biochemistry

  • Substrate sources: glucose from the aqueous, oxygen from the tear film (and from palpebral conjunctival vessels during lid closure).
  • Epithelium: glycogen stores, high pentose phosphate activity (~35%), and anaerobic glycolysis producing lactate. Under contact lens hypoxia lactate accumulates in the stroma, raises osmotic load, and causes stromal oedema and striae.
  • Endothelium: densely packed with mitochondria (second only to the photoreceptor inner segment among ocular tissues) and with Na+/K+ ATPase and carbonic anhydrase: the machinery of deturgescence.
  • Antioxidants: high ascorbate (protecting against UV-B, which the cornea absorbs almost entirely), glutathione, superoxide dismutase, catalase.
  • Collagen: type I predominates in stroma with type V regulating fibril diameter; type IV in basal lamina and Descemet; type VII in anchoring fibrils; type VIII in Descemet.
  • Corneal wound healing biochemistry: epithelial defects heal by sliding then mitosis, requiring fibronectin and an intact basement membrane; stromal wounds recruit keratocytes that transform into fibroblasts and myofibroblasts (TGF-β driven), depositing type III collagen and irregular matrix, hence haze and scar. Matrix metalloproteinases (MMP-9 particularly) mediate melting in dry eye, alkali burns, and rosacea, which is the rationale for doxycycline.

Retinal biochemistry: phototransduction and the visual cycle

Phototransduction: photon to hyperpolarisation
  1. In darkness cGMP is high, cyclic-nucleotide-gated (CNG) channels are open, Na+ and Ca2+ flow inward (the dark current), the photoreceptor sits depolarised near −40 mV, and it tonically releases glutamate.
  2. A photon isomerises 11-cis-retinal to all-trans-retinal within rhodopsin, producing the active intermediate metarhodopsin II.
  3. Metarhodopsin II activates the G protein transducin (exchanging GDP for GTP).
  4. Transducin activates cGMP phosphodiesterase (PDE6).
  5. PDE6 hydrolyses cGMP, so cGMP falls.
  6. CNG channels close, cation influx stops, and the cell hyperpolarises toward −70 mV.
  7. Glutamate release decreases. ON bipolar cells (sign-inverting, mGluR6) depolarise; OFF bipolar cells (sign-conserving) hyperpolarise.
  8. Recovery: falling intracellular Ca2+ activates guanylate cyclase (via GCAP) to remake cGMP; rhodopsin kinase and arrestin quench metarhodopsin II; the RGS9 complex deactivates transducin.
The counter-intuitive core of retinal physiology

Light hyperpolarises the photoreceptor and reduces neurotransmitter release. The photoreceptor is depolarised and busy in the dark. Sildenafil inhibits PDE5 but cross-inhibits PDE6, which is precisely why it can cause transient blue-tinged vision and why it is cautioned in retinitis pigmentosa.

The visual (retinoid) cycle
  1. All-trans-retinal is released from opsin and reduced to all-trans-retinol by retinol dehydrogenase in the photoreceptor.
  2. Retinol crosses to the RPE, carried by interphotoreceptor retinoid-binding protein (IRBP).
  3. In the RPE, LRAT esterifies it to all-trans-retinyl ester.
  4. RPE65 isomerohydrolase converts the ester to 11-cis-retinol.
  5. 11-cis-retinol dehydrogenase oxidises it to 11-cis-retinal.
  6. 11-cis-retinal returns to the photoreceptor and recombines with opsin to regenerate rhodopsin.
  • Vitamin A deficiency therefore causes nyctalopia first (rhodopsin cannot be regenerated), then conjunctival xerosis, Bitot spots (keratinised patches of conjunctival epithelium), keratomalacia, and blindness. It remains the leading cause of preventable childhood blindness worldwide.
  • Mutations: RPE65 causes Leber congenital amaurosis (the target of voretigene neparvovec); ABCA4 failure to clear all-trans-retinal adducts leads to A2E lipofuscin accumulation and Stargardt disease with a dark choroid on angiography.
  • Nutrients concentrated in the retina: taurine is the most abundant free amino acid in the retina and its deficiency causes photoreceptor degeneration; docosahexaenoic acid (DHA, 22:6n-3) makes up a large fraction of outer segment phospholipid; zinc is a cofactor for retinol dehydrogenase and is highly concentrated in RPE and choroid; lutein and zeaxanthin filter blue light and quench singlet oxygen at the macula.

Tears, aqueous, and vitreous composition

Fluid biochemistry: the numbers that get asked
FluidComposition and key values
Tears pH 7.4 (range 6.5–7.6); osmolarity 295–310 mOsm/L, with >308 supporting dry eye; protein 6–10 mg/mL; glucose ~5 mg/dL (too low and too variable for reliable diabetes monitoring). Antibacterial proteins: lysozyme (most abundant, cleaves peptidoglycan), lactoferrin (iron chelation, also antioxidant), lipocalin (tear-specific prealbumin) (lipid binding, and the main protein by concentration in some assays), secretory IgA (the principal immunoglobulin), and defensins. Mucins: gel-forming MUC5AC from goblet cells and membrane-bound MUC1, MUC4, MUC16 from the epithelium.
Aqueous humour Formed at 2.0–3.0 µL/min. Compared with plasma: ascorbate about 20× higher (UV protection); protein about 200× lower (~0.02% versus plasma ~7%), which is the basis of the optically empty anterior chamber and of the Tyndall effect when the barrier breaks; lactate and hydrogen ion higher; glucose about 80% of plasma; amino acids actively transported and above plasma levels; bicarbonate and chloride shifted; TGF-β2 present and immunosuppressive, contributing to anterior chamber associated immune deviation.
Vitreous 98–99% water. Type II collagen fibrils in a hyaluronan matrix, with type IX collagen and opticin regulating spacing. Contains ascorbate, hyaluronidase-resistant proteoglycans, and very little protein. Its high viscosity and slow diffusion govern intravitreal drug half-lives.

AREDS2 formulation: know the exact contents

Vitamin C 500 mg, vitamin E 400 IU, lutein 10 mg and zeaxanthin 2 mg (replacing β-carotene, which increased lung cancer risk in smokers), zinc oxide 80 mg (a 25 mg arm was non-inferior), and cupric oxide 2 mg. Indicated for intermediate AMD in one or both eyes, or advanced AMD in one eye; it reduces progression risk by roughly 25% over five years and does not prevent AMD in people without it.

Key
  1. Aldose reductase; it consumes NADPH, which depletes reduced glutathione and produces oxidative cross-linking of crystallins in addition to osmotic swelling.
  2. Aldose reductase converts galactose to galactitol, for which there is no further dehydrogenase, so it accumulates without limit.
  3. α-crystallin; partially denatured proteins aggregate, become insoluble, and scatter light.
  4. Depolarised (about −40 mV) and tonically releasing glutamate.
  5. Transducin and cGMP phosphodiesterase 6; sildenafil (a PDE5 inhibitor that cross-inhibits PDE6).
  6. RPE65; Leber congenital amaurosis.
  7. Lysozyme, lactoferrin, lipocalin, defensins; secretory IgA.
  8. Protein about 200 times lower (optically clear chamber, and Tyndall flare when the barrier fails) and ascorbate about 20 times higher (UV protection).
  9. Stickler syndrome: optically empty vitreous, high myopia with retinal detachment, cleft palate, deafness, arthropathy.
  10. Type IV collagen; Alport syndrome.
  11. Cystinosis; cysteamine eye drops and systemic cysteamine.
  12. Vitamin C 500 mg, vitamin E 400 IU, lutein 10 mg, zeaxanthin 2 mg, zinc oxide 80 mg, cupric oxide 2 mg; for intermediate AMD in one or both eyes or advanced AMD in one eye.
Chapter recap
  1. Lens: 85% glycolysis, no mitochondria in fibres, pump-leak run from the anterior epithelium.
  2. Polyol pathway causes sugar cataract twice: osmotically through sorbitol and oxidatively through NADPH theft.
  3. α-crystallin is the chaperone; ageing damage is glycation, racemisation, and disulfide cross-linking of proteins that are never replaced.
  4. Cornea gets glucose from aqueous and oxygen from tears; the endothelium is the pump, the epithelium is the barrier.
  5. Light closes CNG channels and reduces glutamate. Transducin then PDE6 then cGMP fall.
  6. Visual cycle turns on RPE65; failure gives Leber congenital amaurosis, and A2E accumulation gives Stargardt.
  7. Tears: lysozyme, lactoferrin, lipocalin, defensins, secretory IgA, MUC5AC; osmolarity above 308 supports dry eye.
  8. Aqueous: ascorbate high, protein 200-fold low, TGF-β2 immunosuppressive.
  9. Collagen II vitreous (Stickler), IV basement membrane (Alport), I stroma (osteogenesis imperfecta), VIII Descemet (Fuchs).
  10. Every classic inborn error has a signature eye finding; learn the enzyme, the metabolite, and the sign as a triple.
