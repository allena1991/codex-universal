The tear (lacrimal) lens

The single most examined concept in rigid lens optics

Between a rigid lens and the cornea sits a tear layer whose power is determined by the mismatch between the lens base curve and the corneal curvature:

Tear lens power = base curve (in D) − flattest K (in D)

  • Fitting steeper than K creates a plus tear lens, so you must add minus to the lens power.
  • Fitting flatter than K creates a minus tear lens, so you must add plus.

SAM / FAP: Steeper Add Minus, Flatter Add Plus.

Each 0.05 mm of base curve change is approximately 0.25 D, and each 0.1 mm is approximately 0.50 D, in the physiologic corneal range.

Worked example. Spectacle Rx −5.00 DS at 12 mm vertex; K readings 43.00 / 43.00. Fit a rigid lens on 42.50 (flatter than K by 0.50 D).
Step 1, vertex conversion: Fcorneal = −5.00 / (1 − 0.012 × −5.00) = −5.00 / 1.06 = −4.72, so −4.75 D.
Step 2, tear lens: fitted flatter, so the tear lens is −0.50 D. FAP: add plus.
Step 3: lens power = −4.75 + 0.50 = −4.25 D.

Residual astigmatism

What a spherical rigid lens corrects, and what it leaves behind

Residual astigmatism = total refractive astigmatism − corneal astigmatism

A spherical rigid gas permeable lens replaces the anterior corneal surface optically, so it neutralises corneal (anterior surface) astigmatism completely. Anything left is internal (lenticular and posterior corneal) astigmatism, and it will persist.

  • Expected residual astigmatism is calculated as above and averages about 0.50 D against the rule in the population (this is what the Javal rule encodes).
  • Induced residual astigmatism arises when a toric back surface rigid lens is used: the tear lens no longer neutralises the toricity evenly, and a front-surface toric compensation (bitoric lens) is needed. The classic solution is the Mandell-Moore or CPE (cylinder power effect) bitoric.
  • Soft lenses drape over the cornea and therefore do not correct corneal astigmatism; a toric soft lens is required for anything above about 0.75–1.00 D.

Example. Refraction −3.00 −2.50 × 180, K readings 42.00 @ 180 / 45.00 @ 090 (corneal astigmatism 3.00 D with the rule, i.e. −3.00 × 180 at the corneal plane). Residual = −2.50 − (−3.00) = +0.50 × 180, equivalent to −0.50 × 090: half a dioptre against the rule will remain with a spherical rigid lens. That is usually acceptable.

Toric soft lens axis correction

LARS

Left Add, Right Subtract. If the lens markings rotate to the patient's left (which is clockwise from your view), add the amount of rotation to the ordered axis; if they rotate to the patient's right, subtract it.

Example. Ordered −2.00 −1.25 × 180; the marking sits 15° rotated to the patient's left. New axis = 180 + 15 = 195, which is 015. Re-order −2.00 −1.25 × 015. Rotation must be stable and repeatable before you compensate; unstable rotation calls for a different stabilisation design instead.

Contact lens materials and oxygen

FDA hydrogel classification and the silicone hydrogel group
GroupWater contentIonic?Behaviour
ILow (<50%)Non-ionicMost deposit-resistant; low Dk; tolerant of all care systems. Example: polymacon.
IIHigh (≥50%)Non-ionicHigher Dk; prone to lipid and protein deposit; sensitive to dehydration.
IIILow (<50%)IonicIntermediate; attracts positively charged deposits (lysozyme).
IVHigh (≥50%)IonicMost deposit-prone and most reactive to preservatives and to hydrogen peroxide residue. Example: etafilcon A.
VSilicone hydrogel (subgroups A, B, C)VariesOxygen is carried by the silicone, not the water, so Dk rises as water content falls: the opposite of conventional hydrogels. Higher modulus (stiffer), which improves handling but predisposes to SEALs, mucin balls, and papillary responses.
  • Dk is a material property: D is diffusivity and k is solubility, in barrers. Dk/t is transmissibility of a specific lens of thickness t, in barrer/cm.
  • Holden-Mertz criteria: Dk/t ≥ 24 to keep daily wear oedema below 4%; Dk/t ≥ 87 to keep overnight oedema at the physiologic 4% level. Harvitt-Bonanno revised these upward to about 35 and 125 to avoid anoxia in any corneal layer.
  • In conventional hydrogels, oxygen moves through water, so Dk increases with water content and decreases with thickness. In silicone hydrogels, the relationship inverts.
  • Rigid gas permeable materials gain Dk from silicone and fluorine content; fluorine improves wettability and deposit resistance. PMMA has a Dk of essentially 0, which is why it is obsolete.
  • Modulus: higher modulus lenses mask astigmatism better and handle more easily but cause more mechanical complications. Wettability is measured by contact angle, lower being better.

Fitting principles

Rigid lens fluorescein patterns
RelationshipFluorescein appearanceConsequence
Alignment (on-K)Thin, even fluorescein across the optic zone with a narrow band of peripheral pooling at the edge liftThe target fit for a spherical cornea: good tear exchange, centration, and comfort.
Steep (apical clearance)Central pooling with a ring of mid-peripheral bearingPoor tear exchange, lens adherence, central corneal oedema and distortion, air bubble trapping. Add plus? No: fitting steep created a plus tear lens, so the lens power needs more minus (SAM).
Flat (apical bearing)Central touch (dark) with peripheral poolingCentral corneal abrasion, excessive movement, low-riding lens, 3 and 9 o'clock staining. Needs more plus (FAP).
With-the-rule toric corneaDumbbell (bowtie) pattern with bearing along the flat horizontal meridianConsider a back-surface toric or a bitoric lens above about 2.00 D of corneal toricity.
KeratoconusTarget three-point touch: light apical touch with two areas of mid-peripheral bearingApical clearance causes bubbles and poor optics; apical bearing causes scarring. Alternatives: scleral, hybrid, or piggyback.
  • Base curve and diameter interact through sagittal depth: increasing the diameter increases the sag, which behaves like a steeper fit, so a common empirical rule is that a 0.5 mm increase in diameter requires flattening the base curve by about 0.05 mm to maintain the same relationship.
  • Soft lens assessment: 0.2–0.5 mm of movement on blink, full corneal coverage with 0.5–1.0 mm of limbal overlap, moderate resistance and smooth recovery on the push-up test, and stable acuity through the blink. A lens that is too tight shows no movement, conjunctival indentation, and initially good then deteriorating vision; too loose shows excessive movement, decentration, and fluctuating vision that clears momentarily after a blink.
  • Scleral lenses: aim for 200–400 µm of central clearance after settling (settling itself is about 100 µm over the first hours), ~50–80 µm of limbal clearance, and an aligned haptic with no blanching or impingement. Watch for midday fogging, conjunctival prolapse, and hypoxic oedema when clearance is excessive.
  • Orthokeratology: a reverse-geometry lens flattens the central cornea by redistributing epithelium (central thinning, mid-peripheral thickening) rather than by reshaping stroma. Corrects up to about −4.00 to −5.00 D and produces peripheral myopic defocus, which is why it slows axial elongation in children by roughly 40–50%. The material risk is microbial keratitis with overnight wear.
  • Presbyopic options: simultaneous vision designs (centre-near or centre-distance aspheric, concentric) present both images at once and rely on neural selection; translating (alternating) designs are rigid, prism-ballasted and truncated, and depend on the lower lid to shift the lens on downgaze; monovision sacrifices stereopsis and is a legitimate but compromise option (with implications for occupational driving).
Contact lens complications: mechanism to sign
ComplicationKey features
Microbial keratitisPain, discharge, epithelial defect over an infiltrate, anterior chamber reaction. Pseudomonas with overnight wear, Acanthamoeba with water exposure. Discontinue lenses, culture if central or large, treat aggressively.
Contact lens acute red eye (CLARE)Sudden unilateral overnight pain and redness with peripheral infiltrates, intact epithelium, from gram-negative endotoxin under a tight lens.
Contact lens peripheral ulcer (CLPU)Small, round, peripheral, full-thickness epithelial loss over a dense infiltrate; sterile, staphylococcal exotoxin-driven; leaves a circular scar.
Infiltrative keratitisMultiple small subepithelial infiltrates with variable staining; a spectrum with the above.
Giant papillary conjunctivitisUpper tarsal papillae above 0.3 mm, mucus, itching, lens awareness and decentration. Mechanical plus immunologic (type I and IV); managed by replacing lenses more frequently, changing material or modality, and mast cell stabilisers.
Superior epithelial arcuate lesion (SEAL)Arcuate superior epithelial split from mechanical shear, associated with high-modulus silicone hydrogels; often asymptomatic.
3 and 9 o'clock stainingPeripheral desiccation with rigid lenses from poor blink and inadequate edge lift; leads to vascularised limbal keratitis if ignored.
Corneal warpageDistorted topography and spectacle blur after lens removal; requires weeks out of lenses before refractive surgery planning.
Neovascularisation and microcystsChronic hypoxia. Microcysts appear at 2 to 6 months of low-Dk wear and paradoxically increase transiently on switching to a higher-Dk lens.
Solution toxicity / hypersensitivityDiffuse punctate staining, especially with hydrogen peroxide that has not neutralised, or preservative sensitivity in group IV materials.
Non-negotiable patient instructions

No water, ever: no rinsing, storing, or swimming in lenses without appropriate protection, because tap water carries Acanthamoeba. Rub and rinse even with "no-rub" solutions. Replace cases every three months. Never top off solution. Overnight wear multiplies microbial keratitis risk several-fold, and smoking multiplies it again.

Low vision optics

Definitions

  • Legal blindness (United States): best-corrected visual acuity of 20/200 or worse in the better eye, or a visual field diameter of 20° or less in the better eye.
  • WHO categories (ICD-11): mild impairment <6/12; moderate <6/18; severe <6/60; blindness <3/60 or a field under 10°.
  • Leading causes in developed countries: age-related macular degeneration, glaucoma, diabetic retinopathy, and cataract; globally, uncorrected refractive error and cataract dominate.

Kestenbaum rule and predicting the add

The required add in dioptres is the reciprocal of the best-corrected distance acuity expressed as a fraction. For 20/200, the add is 200/20 = +10.00 D. For 20/80 it is 80/20 = +4.00 D. This predicts the add needed to read 1 M print (roughly newsprint).

Required magnification = target acuity / current acuity. If a patient sees 20/200 and needs 20/40 to read the target, M = 200/40 = 5×. Then apply a reserve (acuity reserve) factor of about 2× for sustained fluent reading rather than spot reading, because threshold acuity does not support continuous reading.

Equivalent viewing power unifies the devices: EVP = M × 4 (for a 25 cm reference), or EVP = magnifier power × (25 / working distance in cm). Two devices with the same EVP deliver the same retinal image size regardless of whether the magnification comes from a stronger lens or a shorter distance.

The functional interventions that matter as much as the optics
  1. Illumination. Doubling task luminance often improves reading more than adding magnification, especially in AMD. Position the light close, behind and to the side of the better eye.
  2. Contrast. Measure it (Pelli-Robson or Mars) and treat it: reverse polarity, bold print, typoscopes, and dark markings on light backgrounds.
  3. Glare control. Absorptive filters, brimmed hats, side shields, and anti-reflective coatings; distinguish discomfort glare from disability glare.
  4. Eccentric viewing training. Teach the patient to place the image on a preferred retinal locus outside a central scotoma, usually by looking slightly away from the target in a consistent direction, then to build a stable oculomotor habit.
  5. Steady-eye technique. Move the material rather than the eyes, which reduces the demand on eccentric fixation stability.
  6. Set realistic goals with the patient. "Read my medication bottles" and "see my grandchild's face" produce better outcomes than "read again", and they determine which device you prescribe.
  7. Refer. Vision rehabilitation, occupational therapy, orientation and mobility training, state services for the blind, and driving assessment.

Key
  1. Vertex: −8.00 / (1 − 0.012 × −8) = −8.00/1.096 = −7.30, so −7.25 D at the cornea. Fitted steeper by 0.75 D creates a +0.75 tear lens, so SAM: add minus. Lens power = −7.25 − 0.75 = −8.00 D.
  2. Corneal astigmatism is 1.00 D with the rule, i.e. −1.00 × 180. Residual = −1.75 − (−1.00) = −0.75 × 180: three quarters of a dioptre with-the-rule astigmatism will remain, which is enough to blur, so consider a front-surface toric rigid lens or a toric soft lens.
  3. Rotation to the patient's right means subtract: 170 − 10 = axis 160.
  4. Group IV, high water content and ionic: the negative surface charge attracts positively charged proteins such as lysozyme, and high water content admits more material into the matrix.
  5. It falls as water content rises, because oxygen is transported through the silicone phase, so more water means proportionally less silicone.
  6. A steep (apical clearance) fit; the plus tear lens means the lens power needs more minus, and the fit itself should be flattened.
  7. CLARE: overnight, unilateral, peripheral infiltrates with intact epithelium, endotoxin-driven. CLPU: small round peripheral full-thickness epithelial loss over a dense sterile infiltrate, staphylococcal exotoxin. Microbial keratitis: pain, discharge, epithelial defect over the infiltrate with an anterior chamber reaction, and it requires culture and aggressive treatment.
  8. A reverse-geometry lens redistributes epithelium, thinning it centrally and thickening it mid-peripherally, which flattens the central cornea and produces relative peripheral myopic defocus; this slows axial elongation by roughly 40 to 50% in children.
  9. Best-corrected acuity of 20/200 or worse in the better eye, or a visual field of 20 degrees or less in diameter in the better eye.
  10. 250/20 = +12.50 D, implying a working distance of about 8 cm.
  11. 300/50 = 6×; with a 2× acuity reserve for fluent reading, prescribe for about 12× equivalent, which in practice means an electronic device.
  12. The spectacle-mounted high-plus microscope; its drawback is the very short working distance, roughly 1/F, and the need for base-in prism or monocular use.
  13. Base toward the field loss, so base-out on the right eye (and base-in on the left if used binocularly) to shift images from the blind right field into the seeing field.
Chapter recap
  1. Tear lens = base curve minus flattest K. SAM and FAP. 0.05 mm equals 0.25 D.
  2. Residual astigmatism = total minus corneal; a spherical rigid lens corrects only the corneal part.
  3. LARS for toric soft lens axis, but only when rotation is stable.
  4. FDA groups I to IV by water content and ionicity; group IV deposits most; group V silicone hydrogel inverts the water-Dk relationship.
  5. Dk/t 24 daily, 87 overnight, revised to 35 and 125.
  6. Fluorescein: even is aligned, central pool is steep, central touch is flat, bowtie is toric, three-point touch is the keratoconus target.
  7. Never water, always rub and rinse, never sleep in lenses casually.
  8. Legal blindness is 20/200 or a 20 degree field.
  9. Kestenbaum add is the reciprocal of the acuity fraction; then double the magnification for fluent reading.
  10. Light, contrast, glare control, eccentric viewing, and realistic goals often outperform stronger optics.
