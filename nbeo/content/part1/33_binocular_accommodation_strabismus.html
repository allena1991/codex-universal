The AC/A ratio, both ways

Calculated AC/A (in prism dioptres per dioptre):

AC/A = PD(cm) + m × (Δnear − Δdistance)

where m is the near working distance in metres, and deviations use the convention eso positive, exo negative.

Gradient AC/A: change in near deviation per dioptre of added lens power, measured at a fixed distance:

AC/A = (Δwith lens − Δwithout lens) / lens power

The gradient method is the more valid because it holds proximal convergence constant; the calculated method includes proximal convergence and therefore reads higher.

Worked example. PD 60 mm, distance phoria 2 exo (−2), near phoria at 40 cm 10 eso (+10). AC/A = 6 + 0.4 × (10 − (−2)) = 6 + 0.4(12) = 6 + 4.8 = 10.8:1. A high AC/A with near esophoria: this is the pattern of convergence excess, and a plus add is the primary treatment.

Vergence: components and criteria

  • Maddox components of vergence: tonic (baseline innervation from the physiologic position of rest to the distance phoria position), proximal (awareness of nearness), accommodative (via the AC/A relationship), and fusional (disparity-driven, the only one under blur-free feedback control).
  • Fusional vergence has fast and slow components: the fast component responds within ~200 ms; the slow component adapts over minutes and is what shifts the measured phoria after sustained near work.
Clinical criteria for prescribing prism
CriterionStatementApplies to
SheardThe compensating fusional vergence should be at least twice the phoria. Prism = (2 × phoria − compensating fusional reserve) / 3. Exophoria, and generally patients whose symptoms track fusional demand.
PercivalThe demand point should lie in the middle third of the total vergence range. Prism = (G − 2g)/3 where G is the greater and g the lesser limit. Any deviation, and it does not require a phoria measurement; useful when the phoria is unreliable.
1:1 ruleBase-in prism such that the near exophoria equals the base-in break. Prism = (phoria − BI break) / 2. Esophoria at near is not the target; the 1:1 rule is for exophoria, typically divergence excess and basic exo.
Vertical: associated phoriaPrescribe the full associated phoria (fixation disparity measured with a Mallett unit or Wesson card). Vertical deviations, where fusional reserves are small and prism is the primary treatment.

Strabismus classification

Esodeviations
TypeFeatures
Infantile (congenital) esotropia Onset before 6 months, large and stable angle (usually >30 Δ), minimal hyperopia, cross-fixation with apparent abduction deficit, latent nystagmus, inferior oblique overaction, dissociated vertical deviation, and asymmetric monocular optokinetic nystagmus. Early surgical alignment (before 2 years) improves the chance of gross stereopsis.
Refractive accommodative esotropia Onset 2–3 years, significant hyperopia (average +4.00 D), normal AC/A. Deviation equal at distance and near and fully corrected by full cycloplegic plus.
Non-refractive accommodative esotropia (high AC/A) Deviation much greater at near, normal or low hyperopia, high AC/A. Treated with a bifocal add (flat-top segment bisecting the pupil) or, in selected cases, surgery.
Partially accommodative esotropiaSome but not all of the deviation corrected by plus; the residual requires surgery.
Sensory esotropiaUnilateral vision loss in a young child (cataract, corneal opacity, retinoblastoma, optic nerve disease). Always dilate and examine the fundus in any new unilateral strabismus in a child.
Divergence insufficiency / sagging eye syndromeAcquired, comitant, eso greater at distance; in older adults from degeneration of the lateral rectus and superior rectus pulley band. Distinguish from a sixth nerve palsy (incomitant, abduction limited).
Nystagmus blockage syndromeEsotropia that damps the nystagmus; the eye is adducted to use convergence to suppress nystagmus.
Cyclic and acute acquired comitant esotropiaAcute comitant esotropia in an older child or adult mandates neuroimaging to exclude a posterior fossa lesion or Chiari malformation, even though many cases are benign or digital-device associated.

The Parks-Bielschowsky three-step test

Three steps, eight possible answers, one muscle
  1. Which eye is higher in primary gaze? A right hypertropia implicates the depressors of the right eye (RIR, RSO) or the elevators of the left eye (LSR, LIO). That is four muscles.
  2. Is the deviation worse in right or left gaze? Worse in left gaze keeps the muscles that act vertically in left gaze: RSO (depressor in adduction) and LSR (elevator in abduction). That is two muscles.
  3. Bielschowsky head tilt: worse on right or left tilt? On right head tilt, the right eye intorts, which requires the RSO and RSR. If the RSO is weak, the RSR's elevating action is unopposed and the hypertropia worsens. Worse on right tilt therefore indicates the right superior oblique.
Superior oblique palsy: the pattern to recognise instantly

Hypertropia on the affected side that is worse in contralateral gaze, worse on ipsilateral head tilt, and worse in downgaze, with excyclotorsion on double Maddox rod, a compensatory head tilt away from the affected side, and often a V pattern. It is the commonest isolated cyclovertical palsy and the commonest cause of acquired vertical diplopia. Congenital cases have large vertical fusional amplitudes (>3 Δ), a long-standing head tilt, and facial asymmetry.

Bilateral superior oblique palsy shows a hypertropia that reverses in lateral gaze, a V pattern, and excyclotorsion greater than 10°: think head trauma.

Beware the mimics of a three-step test: skew deviation (which shows a conjugate ocular tilt reaction with head tilt toward the lower eye and incyclotorsion of that eye), restrictive disease, myasthenia, and previous strabismus surgery all break the assumptions of the test.

Sensory adaptations and amblyopia

  • Suppression: active cortical inhibition of one image. Central suppression scotoma in monofixation syndrome, hemiretinal suppression in exotropia.
  • Anomalous retinal correspondence: a shifted relationship between the two retinas producing a shifted horopter; diagnosed when the objective angle differs from the subjective angle (the angle of anomaly).
  • Eccentric fixation: a non-foveal retinal point is used for monocular fixation; measured with a visuoscope. Distinguish from eccentric viewing, which is a deliberate strategy in low vision.
  • Monofixation syndrome: a small (usually ≤8 Δ) deviation with peripheral fusion, a central suppression scotoma, gross but not fine stereopsis, and often mild amblyopia. Frequently the best achievable outcome after infantile strabismus surgery.
  • Panum fusional area permits fusion of slightly disparate images; it is narrowest at the fovea (about 6–10 arcmin) and widens with eccentricity, which is why peripheral fusion survives when central fusion is lost.
  • Physiologic diplopia is normal and is the basis of much vergence therapy.

PEDIG and ATS evidence: the trial results that get examined
  • Refractive adaptation first. Spectacles alone improve amblyopia in a large fraction of children, and improvement continues for up to 30 weeks. Do not start patching before giving glasses an adequate trial.
  • Patching dose. 2 hours per day is as effective as 6 hours for moderate amblyopia (20/40 to 20/80), and 6 hours is as effective as full-time for severe amblyopia (20/100 to 20/400).
  • Atropine. 1% daily is equivalent to patching for moderate amblyopia, and weekend-only atropine is equivalent to daily. It is a legitimate first-line alternative, with better adherence in many families.
  • Age. Children aged 7–12 still respond, and 13–17-year-olds respond if previously untreated. Age alone is not a reason to withhold treatment.
  • Near activities during patching add little measurable benefit over patching alone in the trials, though they aid compliance.
  • Recurrence occurs in about a quarter of children after stopping; taper rather than stop abruptly, and follow for at least a year.
  • Binocular (dichoptic) treatments, including digital therapeutics, are an active area and show benefit in some trials, particularly in older children.

Key
  1. AC/A = 6.4 + 0.4(−12 − 0) = 6.4 − 4.8 = 1.6:1, a low AC/A with near exophoria: convergence insufficiency.
  2. Base-in x/13/21/13 (blur/break/recovery reported as 13/21/13) and base-out 17/21/11.
  3. Sheard: the compensating reserve should be twice the phoria. Prism = (2 × 10 − 14)/3 = 6/3 = 2 Δ base-in.
  4. Sheard for exophoria; Percival is general and is often preferred for esophoria.
  5. Refractive accommodative esotropia with a normal AC/A.
  6. Non-refractive accommodative esotropia (high AC/A); treat with a plus add at near, typically a flat-top bifocal set to bisect the pupil.
  7. Right superior oblique.
  8. Skew deviation shows a conjugate ocular tilt reaction with head tilt toward the lower eye, incyclotorsion of the lower eye (whereas SO palsy gives excyclotorsion of the higher eye), and accompanying brainstem or cerebellar signs; the hypertropia is often comitant.
  9. Restriction: positive forced duction, IOP rise on gaze into the field of limitation, and normal saccadic velocity with an abrupt stop. Paresis: negative forced duction, no IOP change, and slowed saccadic velocity.
  10. It compares the brightness of the two fundus reflexes; the brighter reflex belongs to the abnormal (deviating, amblyopic, or more ametropic) eye.
  11. Random-dot tests (Randot, TNO, Frisby) require bifoveal fusion; a monofixator typically achieves gross stereopsis of about 70–3000 arcsec on contour tests and fails fine random-dot levels.
  12. Two hours daily for moderate amblyopia (equivalent to six), six hours for severe (equivalent to full-time); atropine 1% is equivalent to patching for moderate amblyopia, and weekend-only dosing equals daily.
  13. Spasmus nutans; exclude a chiasmal or hypothalamic glioma with neuroimaging.
  14. Arnold-Chiari malformation and a foramen magnum or cervicomedullary lesion; lithium (or anticonvulsant) toxicity.
Chapter recap
  1. Morgan norms are a memorisation task; the exam expects the numbers, not approximations.
  2. AC/A calculated = PD + m(Δnear − Δdistance) with eso positive; the gradient method is more valid.
  3. Sheard for exo, Percival broadly, 1:1 for exo at near, full associated phoria for verticals.
  4. High AC/A with near eso means plus add; low AC/A with near exo means vergence therapy (CITT).
  5. Esotropia types are separated by hyperopia and the near-distance relationship; sensory esotropia demands a dilated fundus exam.
  6. A pattern goes with superior oblique overaction, V pattern with inferior oblique overaction.
  7. Three-step test: higher eye, worse gaze, worse tilt; but check for skew, restriction, and myasthenia.
  8. Random-dot stereo needs bifoveal fusion; monofixators pass contour tests only.
  9. Glasses first, then 2 hours for moderate and 6 for severe, or atropine; older children still respond.
  10. Nystagmus type localises: downbeat cervicomedullary, upbeat medullary, seesaw parasellar, opsoclonus paraneoplastic.
