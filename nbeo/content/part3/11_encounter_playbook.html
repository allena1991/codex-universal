Three timing rules that decide the station
  1. Cap the history at four minutes. A thorough focused history is scored; an exhaustive one costs you the note. Four minutes is enough for a complete focused history if you have rehearsed the sequence.
  2. Start the note by minute 11 at the latest. The note carries 54% of the blueprint. Leaving under four minutes for it is the single most common self-inflicted failure.
  3. Do the patient education before you type. If you type first and run out of time, you lose Patient Education and Communication, which together are 24%. If you educate first and run short on typing, you can still capture the essentials in bullet form.

The focused case history

The case history is explicitly part of the Skills sub-score, and it is scored on what you verbalise. You do not need to enter subjective information into the note. That means you must actually ask the questions out loud, not simply infer the answers.

The six categories, and what to ask in each
CategoryAsk
1. History of the present illness Location ("Which eye, or both?"), onset ("When did it start? Was it sudden or gradual?"), duration ("How long has it lasted? Is it constant or does it come and go?"), severity ("On a scale of one to ten?" or "How much is it affecting what you do?"), frequency ("How often?"), modifying factors ("What makes it better or worse?"), associated symptoms ("Anything else with it: pain, redness, discharge, flashes, floaters, headache, nausea?"), prior episodes ("Has this happened before? What was done?"), and further description ("Describe it in your own words.").
2. Past ocular history "Have you ever been diagnosed with any eye condition?" plus, for each: how long, and what treatment. Then: eye surgery or laser, eye injury, date of your last eye examination, do you wear glasses or contact lenses, what type, how old is the prescription, and how compliant are you with wear and replacement. For contact lens cases: wear schedule, overnight wear, solution, water exposure, replacement interval, and comfort.
3. Medical history and medications "Do you have any medical conditions?" and for each: how long and what treatment and how well controlled. Date of last physical examination. All medications including over-the-counter, supplements, eye drops, inhalers, injections, and creams. Allergies, and what reaction. Then relevant specifics: for a diabetic, the most recent HgbA1c; for a hypertensive, the most recent blood pressure.
4. Surgical history"Any surgeries or procedures, on the eyes or elsewhere?" and when.
5. Family historyFamily eye history: glaucoma, macular degeneration, retinal detachment, blindness, strabismus or lazy eye, keratoconus, and any inherited eye disease. Family medical history: diabetes, hypertension, heart disease, stroke, autoimmune disease, and cancer.
6. Social history Occupation and the visual demands of it. Hobbies. Driving. Smoking, alcohol, and recreational drug use. Risky behaviours where relevant, including sexual history when the differential includes a sexually transmitted infection. Eye protection use at work and in sport. Living situation and support, where relevant to management.
A rehearsed opening and history sequence Good morning, Ms. Lee. My name is candidate [OE TRACKER number] and I am the optometrist seeing you today. I am going to ask you some questions about your eyes and your health, then review your test results, and then we will talk about what I find and what we will do. Is that all right? Tell me in your own words what brought you in today. Which eye is it, or is it both? When did it start? Was it sudden or gradual? Is it constant or does it come and go? On a scale of one to ten, how bad is it? Does anything make it better or worse? Have you noticed anything else along with it, such as pain, redness, discharge, flashing lights, floaters, or headache? Has this ever happened before? Have you ever been told you have any eye condition? Have you had any eye surgery, laser, or injury? When was your last eye examination? Do you wear glasses or contact lenses, and how old is that prescription? Do you have any medical conditions? When was your last physical? What medications are you taking, including anything over the counter, supplements, and any eye drops? Do you have any allergies, and what happens when you take them? Have you had any surgeries or procedures? Does anyone in your family have eye problems such as glaucoma, macular degeneration, or a retinal detachment? Any family history of diabetes, high blood pressure, or autoimmune disease? What do you do for work, and what are your eyes doing all day? Do you drive? Any hobbies? Do you smoke, or drink alcohol? Do you wear eye protection at work or for sport?
History mistakes that cost items
  • Asking closed compound questions. "Any pain, redness, or discharge?" often gets a single answer covering only one element. Ask them separately when the differential depends on them.
  • Skipping medication and allergy questions because the list is already on the device. The list on the device is data; asking is a scored behaviour, and it also lets you ask about compliance, over-the-counter agents, and drops that may not be listed.
  • Not asking about compliance and prescription age in a refractive or contact lens case.
  • Omitting social history because it feels irrelevant. Occupation, driving, smoking, and eye protection are scored and frequently change the management.
  • Failing to ask follow-up questions after seeing the data. You may question the SP at any time in the cycle. Once you have the clinical data, one or two targeted questions often clinch the diagnosis, and this is a strong candidate behaviour.
  • Talking to the screen instead of the patient. Communication is scored by the SP.

Clinical data on the device

  • Becomes available the moment the cycle begins and remains until the cycle ends.
  • Contains, where relevant to the case: pretest information, refractive information, anterior segment evaluation, and posterior segment evaluation, plus the overview information you already read.
  • You may move between tabs freely: clinical data, ancillary tests, and assessment and plan.
  • You may review data, ask questions, order tests, and write the note in any order.
  • Read it actively. Some findings will be normal and some abnormal, and it is your job to identify which. Numbers are given without interpretation.
How to read the clinical data in ninety seconds
  1. Acuity and pupils first. Reduced acuity plus an afferent defect changes the whole differential toward optic nerve or extensive retinal disease.
  2. IOP next. A number outside the teens reframes the case immediately.
  3. Then the section that matches the chief complaint, in detail.
  4. Then everything else, scanning for the one abnormal line. Cases are constructed with a small number of deliberate abnormal findings; find them all before committing.
  5. Then ask yourself the negative question: what is conspicuously absent that would have supported my leading diagnosis? Absent findings are as informative as present ones.
  6. Finally, reconcile the data with the history. If they do not fit, you have the wrong diagnosis, or there is a second one.

Ordering ancillary tests

The rules, from the candidate guide
RuleDetail
You type the testsThere is no menu. Type the test name and an indication for each.
Be as specific as you would be clinically"Macular OCT" not "OCT". "Westergren ESR" not "inflammatory markers". "CN V assessment" for corneal sensation.
Grouped panels are acceptable when they are clinically ordered as a groupA CBC or a metabolic panel may be ordered as such. Anything normally ordered individually, such as a specific antibody or antigen test, must be listed separately. If unsure, list separately.
Give an indicationEach test needs a reason: "Westergren ESR, indication: rule out giant cell arteritis"; "Macular OCT, indication: evaluate for choroidal neovascular membrane"; "Contact lens fitting and assessment, indication: determine whether contact lenses will improve acuity".
Assume full scope and full accessYou may order any test, laboratory investigation, or imaging study, on the understanding that you practise at full scope and the patient has access to it.
Submission is finalOnce you select "Submit ancillary tests" you cannot add any more in that cycle. Write the full list before submitting.
You may receive results you did not orderThe results provided are those associated with the case and may not match your list. Receiving an unordered result does not mean you lost points: you are scored on what you ordered, and multiple test sets can be correct. Some results will be normal, and recognising that is part of the task.

Patient education, spoken

Five things to say, in this order
  1. What it is. Name the diagnosis (the one place medical terminology is expected), then immediately translate it. "You have what we call open-angle glaucoma. That means the drainage system inside your eye is not working efficiently, so the pressure is a little high and it is slowly damaging the nerve that carries the picture from your eye to your brain."
  2. Why it happened. The cause or mechanism, in plain language and without blame. "We do not know exactly why it starts, but it runs in families and it becomes commoner with age."
  3. What we are going to do. The treatment, with how and when. "I am going to start you on a drop called latanoprost. One drop in each eye every night at bedtime. It works by helping fluid drain out of the eye. Your eyes may look a little red at first and your lashes may grow longer over time."
  4. What to watch for. Warning signs that mean come back sooner. "If you notice pain, redness that does not settle, or a sudden change in vision, I need to see you straight away."
  5. When to come back. A specific interval. "I want to see you again in six weeks to check that the pressure has come down."

Then check understanding: "Just so I know I have explained it clearly, can you tell me how you are going to use the drop?" This is worth doing every time: it is good communication, it is scored, and it catches misunderstanding.

Language rules. Name the diagnosis, then drop the jargon entirely. Do not say "intraocular pressure", "neovascularisation", "posterior vitreous detachment", "photocoagulation", or "topical". Say pressure inside the eye, new abnormal blood vessels, the jelly inside the eye pulling away, laser treatment, and eye drops. Avoid frightening absolutes, but do not soften a serious diagnosis into meaninglessness either: the patient needs to understand why the follow-up matters.

Chapter recap
  1. Four minutes of history, note started by minute eleven, education before typing.
  2. Six history categories, asked out loud, because the history is scored on verbalisation.
  3. Read the data actively: acuity and pupils, IOP, the relevant section, then hunt for every abnormal line.
  4. Ask follow-up questions after reading the data.
  5. Type specific tests with specific indications, and submit only once, because submission is final.
  6. An unordered result appearing is not a sign you were wrong.
  7. Education has five parts and ends with a check of understanding.
  8. Name the diagnosis, then abandon the jargon entirely.
  9. Dismiss the SP explicitly and only when you are truly finished with them.
  10. Speak to the patient, not the screen.
