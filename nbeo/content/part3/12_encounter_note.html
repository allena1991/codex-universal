The assessment

Rules for the assessment field
RuleDetail
Selected, not typed freelyType into the box and a drop-down populates from the Part III PEPS Diagnosis List; select your intended diagnosis from it. Familiarise yourself with the published diagnosis list before exam day, so you know the wording the platform uses.
Location is mandatoryOptions are OD, OS, OU, and N/A. Location refers to where the condition manifests in the eye or eyes. Use N/A when the diagnosis is not an ocular structure diagnosis, for example a systemic condition.
Choose the most significant diagnosis of today's visitInformed by the chief complaint, the HPI, the history, and the clinical data. Not the incidental finding, and not a list of everything you noticed.
Be as specific as the data allows"Primary open-angle glaucoma, OU" rather than "glaucoma". "Rhegmatogenous retinal detachment, OD" rather than "retinal detachment". Specificity is what the assessment domain rewards.
Assessment errors
  • Choosing a symptom instead of a diagnosis. "Blurred vision" or "floaters" is not an assessment.
  • Choosing the incidental finding. A patient with a chief complaint of a curtain and a detachment does not have an assessment of "posterior vitreous detachment".
  • Leaving the location blank or defaulting to OU when the disease is unilateral.
  • Being vaguer than the data supports. If the case gives you an ischaemic CRVO, say ischaemic.
  • Hedging with two diagnoses when the case points clearly to one. Commit.

The plan

Five components, every time
  1. Treatment: medical therapy. Drug, concentration, route, frequency, and duration, with instructions on use. Generic or brand names are both acceptable. For a patient 18 years or younger you may write "prescribed at pediatric dose" rather than calculating it.
  2. Treatment: non-medical therapy. Refractive prescriptions written out in full, or referenced explicitly to the data ("prescribe the manifest refraction as documented", "prescribe the cycloplegic refraction OU"), with instructions on wear. Also: lid hygiene, compresses, contact lens changes, prism, occlusion, vision therapy, lifestyle change, and protective eyewear.
  3. Referrals. To whom, for what, and in what timeframe. "Refer to ophthalmology" is explicitly unacceptable. "Refer to a retinal specialist for repair of a retinal detachment within 2 days" is the standard the guide sets.
  4. Additional testing. Any further investigation required by the standard of care, with the rule-in or rule-out indication.
  5. Patient education, written in patient-centred language, including the diagnosis and its cause or mechanism, plus the pertinent additional information you gave: home care, activity modification, alternatives, medication instructions and side effects, prognosis, warning signs, and any urgent action.
  6. Return to clinic. A single specific interval: 1 day, 1 week, 1 month, 6 months. No ranges ("3 to 6 months") and no calendar dates ("January 1").

Punctuation, grammar, and spelling do not affect scoring, provided the documentation would be acceptable in a medical record. Content and completeness are what is scored. The note autosaves and submits automatically at the end of the cycle, and cannot be edited afterwards.

A reusable plan template

Type this skeleton, then fill it

TREATMENT
Medical: [drug] [concentration] [route], [frequency], [duration]. Instructions: [how to use].
Non-medical: [spectacles / contact lens / prism / occlusion / therapy / lid hygiene / protective eyewear], with instructions.

REFERRAL
Refer to [specific specialist] for [specific purpose] within [specific timeframe]. [Method: telephoned today / letter sent.]

ADDITIONAL TESTING
[Test], indication: [rule in or rule out what].

PATIENT EDUCATION
Explained that the patient has [diagnosis], which means [plain-language mechanism]. Cause: [plain-language aetiology].

Home care and activity: [instructions].
Warning signs requiring immediate return: [list].
Prognosis discussed: [statement].
Alternatives discussed: [option].
Patient verbalised understanding.

Six worked notes

Worked note 1 · Primary open-angle glaucoma

Assessment: Primary open-angle glaucoma · OU

TREATMENT
Medical: latanoprost 0.005% ophthalmic solution, 1 drop each eye at bedtime, continuing indefinitely. Instructions: instil one drop in each eye at night, close the lids gently and press at the inner corner for 2 minutes, wait 5 minutes between any other drops, and do not skip doses.
Non-medical: discussed selective laser trabeculoplasty as an alternative first-line option.
REFERRAL
None required at present. Will refer to a glaucoma specialist if target pressure is not achieved on maximal tolerated therapy or if progression occurs.
ADDITIONAL TESTING
Threshold visual field 24-2 both eyes, indication: establish a second reliable baseline. OCT retinal nerve fibre layer and ganglion cell analysis, indication: structural baseline. Central corneal thickness pachymetry, indication: interpret applanation pressure and assess risk. Optic disc photography, indication: baseline documentation. Gonioscopy, indication: confirm an open angle and exclude secondary mechanisms.
PATIENT EDUCATION
Explained that the patient has open-angle glaucoma, which means the drainage system inside the eye is not working efficiently, so the pressure is higher than the optic nerve can tolerate and the nerve is being slowly damaged. Cause: not fully known, but risk rises with age, family history, and higher pressure. Explained that the damage is painless and affects side vision first, which is why it is usually found on examination rather than noticed. Target pressure discussed. Treatment explained as above, including that the drop may cause redness, longer and darker lashes, and gradual darkening of the iris colour. Emphasised that treatment prevents further loss but cannot restore vision already lost, and that daily adherence is what protects the remaining vision. Warning signs requiring immediate return: pain, marked redness, or sudden change in vision. Advised that first-degree relatives should have their pressures and optic nerves checked. Patient verbalised understanding and restated the dosing.
RTC
6 weeks.

Worked note 2 · Rhegmatogenous retinal detachment

Assessment: Rhegmatogenous retinal detachment · OD

TREATMENT
Medical: none. Nil by mouth in anticipation of surgery.
Non-medical: instructed the patient to limit activity and to position so that the superotemporal break is dependent, to limit progression of fluid toward the central vision.
REFERRAL
Refer to a vitreoretinal surgeon for surgical repair of a macula-on rhegmatogenous retinal detachment with a superotemporal horseshoe tear, within 24 hours. Telephoned the on-call retina service today and spoke with the surgeon; patient is being seen this afternoon. Written summary and images sent with the patient.
ADDITIONAL TESTING
Scleral indented dilated fundus examination of the fellow eye, indication: identify any additional break requiring prophylactic treatment. B-scan ultrasonography if the view is obscured, indication: define the extent of detachment. Fundus photography, indication: documentation.
PATIENT EDUCATION
Explained that the retina, the light-sensitive lining at the back of the eye, has a tear and is peeling away, and that fluid is collecting underneath it. Cause: the jelly inside the eye has pulled away with age and short-sightedness, and in doing so it tore the retina. Explained that this requires surgery urgently, today, because the detachment has not yet reached the central vision and repairing it before it does gives the best chance of good vision. Explained the positioning instruction and the reason for it, and that he should not eat or drink until the surgical team has assessed him. Explained that the fellow eye is at increased risk and will be examined and monitored. Warning signs: any further loss of vision before he is seen, in which case he should go directly to the emergency department. Patient verbalised understanding and repeated the positioning instruction.
RTC
1 day.

Worked note 3 · Bacterial corneal ulcer, contact lens related

Assessment: Bacterial keratitis · OS

TREATMENT
Medical: moxifloxacin 0.5% ophthalmic solution, 1 drop OS every hour around the clock for 48 hours, then taper according to response. Homatropine 5% ophthalmic solution, 1 drop OS three times daily for pain and to prevent posterior synechiae. Oral acetaminophen as needed for pain. No topical corticosteroid at this stage.
Non-medical: discontinue all contact lens wear immediately; discard the current lenses, case, and solution; do not resume wear until authorised.
REFERRAL
Refer to a corneal specialist within 24 hours if the infiltrate enlarges, if there is corneal thinning, or if there is no improvement at the 24-hour review.
ADDITIONAL TESTING
Corneal scraping from the edge and base of the ulcer for Gram stain and culture on blood, chocolate, and Sabouraud agar plus non-nutrient agar with an E. coli overlay, indication: identify the organism including Acanthamoeba and fungi. Culture of the contact lens, case, and solution, indication: identify the source organism. Corneal sensation testing (CN V assessment), indication: exclude a herpetic or neurotrophic component. Anterior segment photography, indication: document size and depth for comparison.
PATIENT EDUCATION
Explained that there is an infected ulcer on the clear front window of the eye, caused by bacteria that got in under the contact lens, and that sleeping in lenses and rinsing them in tap water greatly increase this risk. Explained that this is serious and can scar the cornea and permanently reduce vision if not treated intensively, which is why the drops are needed every hour, including through the night, and why I need to see her again tomorrow. Explained the cycloplegic drop will blur her near vision and enlarge the pupil for a day or so. Discussed lens hygiene in detail for the future: no water contact of any kind, rub and rinse, replace the case every three months, no overnight wear, and never top off solution. Warning signs requiring immediate return: worsening pain, increasing redness, more discharge, or any further drop in vision. Advised she must not drive while the vision is reduced. Patient verbalised understanding of the hourly dosing including overnight.
RTC
1 day.

Worked note 4 · Refractive case with accommodative esotropia in a child

Assessment: Refractive accommodative esotropia · OU

TREATMENT
Medical: none.
Non-medical: prescribe the full cycloplegic refraction as documented in the clinical data, OU, in polycarbonate lenses, for full-time wear. Do not reduce the plus. Spectacles to be worn from waking to bedtime including for all near work.
REFERRAL
Refer to a paediatric ophthalmologist for co-management within 4 weeks if the deviation is not fully eliminated by the full hyperopic correction, for consideration of surgery for any residual angle.
ADDITIONAL TESTING
Cover test at distance and near with and without the correction, indication: quantify the residual deviation on correction. Stereoacuity testing, indication: assess binocular function. Monocular acuity with an age-appropriate optotype, indication: detect amblyopia. Dilated fundus examination, indication: exclude a sensory cause of the deviation such as an optic nerve or macular abnormality. AC/A determination, indication: identify a high AC/A that would require a near add.
PATIENT EDUCATION
Explained to the parent that the child is significantly long-sighted, and that the amount of focusing effort needed to see clearly is pulling the eye inward. Cause: the eye is shorter than average, so it has to focus hard even for distance, and focusing and turning inward are linked in the brain. Explained that glasses do the focusing work so the eye no longer needs to turn in, and that the eye turn should improve substantially or resolve with full-time wear. Emphasised that the glasses must be worn all day, that the eye will turn in again when they are removed and this is expected, and that under-correcting the glasses would allow the turn to return. Explained the risk of amblyopia in the turning eye and that we will monitor acuity, and that patching may be needed. Explained that the prescription will be rechecked regularly as the child grows. Warning signs: worsening or constant turn, or the child covering or closing one eye. Parent verbalised understanding of the requirement for full-time wear.
RTC
6 weeks.

Worked note 5 · Neuro-ophthalmic case requiring same-day action

Assessment: Giant cell arteritis · N/A (with arteritic anterior ischaemic optic neuropathy, OS)

TREATMENT
Medical: oral prednisone 60 mg (1 mg/kg) given today as the first dose, with immediate arrangement for intravenous methylprednisolone 1 g daily for 3 days given the visual loss. Gastric protection with a proton pump inhibitor. Treatment started before biopsy.
Non-medical: advised the patient not to drive.
REFERRAL
Refer to the emergency department today, immediately, for admission, intravenous methylprednisolone, and urgent temporal artery biopsy. Telephoned the on-call medical team and the rheumatology service today and spoke directly with the physician. Also refer to rheumatology for ongoing steroid management and consideration of tocilizumab as a steroid-sparing agent.
ADDITIONAL TESTING
Westergren ESR, indication: rule in giant cell arteritis. CRP, indication: same. Platelet count and CBC, indication: thrombocytosis and anaemia support the diagnosis. Temporal artery biopsy, indication: confirm the diagnosis histologically; remains informative for approximately 2 weeks after starting steroid. Threshold visual field both eyes, indication: baseline. OCT retinal nerve fibre layer, indication: baseline. Blood pressure, fasting glucose, and HgbA1c, indication: vascular risk and steroid monitoring.
PATIENT EDUCATION
Explained that the loss of vision has been caused by inflammation of the blood vessels that supply the optic nerve, a condition called giant cell arteritis, and that it is a whole-body condition, not just an eye condition. Cause: the immune system is attacking the walls of medium-sized arteries. Explained that this is an emergency because the other eye is at high risk of losing vision within days if it is not treated immediately, and that high-dose steroid started today is what protects it. Explained that the vision already lost in the left eye is unlikely to recover. Explained the steroid side effects to expect and that she must not stop it herself. Explained the biopsy and that it can still be done after starting treatment. Warning signs requiring immediate return or emergency attendance: any change in the vision of the right eye, double vision, or severe headache. Advised not to drive. Patient verbalised understanding of the urgency and is being taken to the emergency department now.
RTC
1 day.

Worked note 6 · Systemic disease case

Assessment: Severe non-proliferative diabetic retinopathy with centre-involving diabetic macular oedema · OU

TREATMENT
Medical: intravitreal anti-VEGF therapy (aflibercept) to be initiated for centre-involving macular oedema, by the retina service. No topical therapy indicated.
Non-medical: do not prescribe new spectacles at this time because the refraction is unstable at an HgbA1c of 9.4%; recheck when glycaemic control is stable.
REFERRAL
Refer to a retinal specialist for intravitreal anti-VEGF therapy for centre-involving diabetic macular oedema, within 2 weeks. Refer to the primary care physician within 1 week for intensification of glycaemic and blood pressure management; written summary sent describing an HgbA1c of 9.4%, blood pressure of 148/88, and severe non-proliferative retinopathy in both eyes, and requesting reassessment of therapy.
ADDITIONAL TESTING
Macular OCT both eyes, indication: quantify central subfield thickness and monitor response. Fundus photography, indication: document severity for comparison. Fluorescein angiography or OCT angiography, indication: assess macular ischaemia and identify areas of capillary non-perfusion. HgbA1c and lipid panel, indication: systemic risk assessment. Urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio, indication: screen for nephropathy. Blood pressure, indication: independent risk factor for progression.
PATIENT EDUCATION
Explained that diabetes has damaged the small blood vessels at the back of both eyes, that these vessels are leaking fluid into the central part of the retina, which is why reading has become harder, and that the changes are now at the stage where about half of people go on to grow abnormal new blood vessels within a year if nothing changes. Cause: prolonged high blood sugar damages the walls of the smallest vessels. Explained that injections into the eye are the most effective treatment for the swelling, and what the injection involves. Explained clearly that improving blood sugar and blood pressure control changes the course of this disease and is as important as the injections, and that I have written to his physician today. Explained why I am not changing his glasses yet. Warning signs requiring urgent return: a sudden shower of floaters, a curtain across the vision, or a sudden drop in vision, which could mean bleeding or a detachment. Discussed that he should tell me if he becomes pregnant is not applicable; advised annual dilated examination is now insufficient and he needs review every 3 to 4 months. Patient verbalised understanding.
RTC
1 month.

Plan errors that cost the Management and Documentation domain
  • A drug with no concentration, frequency, or duration.
  • "Refer to ophthalmology" with no subspecialty, reason, or timeframe. This is named as unacceptable in the candidate guide.
  • A range for the return-to-clinic interval, or a calendar date.
  • No patient education section, or a section that simply says "educated patient".
  • No warning signs for a condition that can deteriorate.
  • Missing the systemic referral in a case where the eye finding reveals systemic disease.
  • Failing to document that treatment was started when you started it, and the time.
  • Writing "refractive correction" without either the numbers or an explicit reference to the manifest or cycloplegic data.
  • Forgetting non-medical therapy entirely: lid hygiene, lens discontinuation, positioning, prism, occlusion, protective eyewear, and lifestyle change all count.
  • Running out of time. If you have two minutes left, type in this order: treatment, referral with timeframe, RTC, then education. Those four earn the most.

Key
  1. Typed into a box that populates a drop-down from the Part III PEPS Diagnosis List, from which you select. Locations: OD, OS, OU, N/A.
  2. When the diagnosis does not relate to the eye or an ocular structure, for example a systemic condition.
  3. The most significant diagnosis found at today's visit, informed by the chief complaint, HPI, history, and clinical data.
  4. Medical therapy; non-medical therapy; referrals; additional testing; patient education in patient-centred language; and a return-to-clinic interval.
  5. To whom, for what, and by when. Unacceptable: "Refer to ophthalmology." Acceptable: "Refer to a retina specialist for the repair of a retinal detachment within 2 days."
  6. "Prescribed at pediatric dose" is acceptable for a patient 18 years or younger.
  7. Either write the numbers out, or reference the specific data explicitly ("prescribe the cycloplegic refraction as documented, OU"), with instructions on wear.
  8. No ranges and no calendar dates: a single specific interval such as 1 day, 1 week, 1 month, or 6 months.
  9. No, provided the documentation would be acceptable in a medical record.
  10. No. It autosaves and submits automatically, and cannot be edited.
  11. Treatment, referral with timeframe, RTC, then patient education.
  12. "Refer to a vitreoretinal surgeon for surgical repair of a macula-on rhegmatogenous retinal detachment within 24 hours; on-call retina service telephoned today."

Chapter recap
  1. The note carries 54% of the blueprint. Practise typing it under time.
  2. Select the assessment from the diagnosis list and always set the location, using N/A for non-ocular diagnoses.
  3. Enter the most significant diagnosis of the visit, as specifically as the data allows.
  4. Every plan needs medical therapy, non-medical therapy, referrals, additional testing, education, and an RTC.
  5. Referrals need a named specialist, a reason, and a timeframe.
  6. Doses need concentration, frequency, and duration; pediatric doses may be written as "prescribed at pediatric dose".
  7. RTC is a single interval, never a range and never a date.
  8. Patient education in the note must be in patient-centred language and must include the mechanism.
  9. Include warning signs for anything that can deteriorate.
  10. With time running out: treatment, referral, RTC, education.
