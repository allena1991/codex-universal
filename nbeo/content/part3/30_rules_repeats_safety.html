Views: obtaining, confirming, and troubleshooting

The rules on views, precisely as the guide states them
RuleDetail
Cameras The slit lamp camera is mounted on the left ocular; you are scored on what the camera captures through the left ocular, so ensure the image is fully seen there. The BIO camera is mounted centrally, with images recorded through each ocular. Cameras, monitors, and recordings are calibrated, so if the view is not obstructed, the recorded image is exactly what you see.
You are responsible for the viewIt is your responsibility to ensure the examiner or proctor has a view. Nobody will volunteer that they do not.
AskingYou may ask at any point while performing the view. You will be told either "I have a view" or "I do not have a view".
What confirmation means Only that something is visible. It does not tell you the quality of the view, whether it meets the minimum criteria for the item, or whether it is of the correct structure, angle, or quadrant.
Asking afterwardsUseless. You will be told "I can only comment on a view while it is being performed."
If told there is no viewTroubleshoot, and specifically ensure nothing obstructs the left ocular of the slit lamp or the central camera of the BIO headset: your hand, the lens edge, a lid, lashes, the condensing lens rim, or your own head position.
MonitorsThere is a monitor in the room for the examiner or proctor only. You may not look at it at any time; attempting to do so may be treated as improper conduct.
Fleeting viewsDo not count. Hold the view long enough for the examiner to see and score it, and while you hold it, describe it.
"Entire"Means 360 degrees or the complete structure. Sweep and say that you are sweeping.
A troubleshooting sequence to run automatically
  1. Ask, while performing: "Do you have a view?" Do it early rather than after four failed attempts.
  2. Check for obstruction. Move your hand, fingers, and the lens rim out of the optical path; move your head so you are not blocking the ocular; check the patient's lashes and lids are not in the way.
  3. Reset the basics. Confirm the patient's chin and forehead are in position, confirm gaze direction, and confirm your own focus and illumination.
  4. Change one variable at a time. With the fundus lens or condensing lens, change the lens-to-eye distance first, then the slit lamp position, then the beam width. Changing three things at once loses the reference point.
  5. Re-find and re-withdraw. With BIO or the fundus lens, move closer until the image appears, then withdraw slowly until it fills the lens.
  6. Re-instruct the patient. Most lost views are a gaze problem, not an optics problem. "Look further up for me, all the way, as if you are trying to see your own eyebrows."
  7. Move on if you have used two attempts. An item is worth one point; the remaining items are worth many. You will be stopped after four attempts, and a stop scores every remaining item in that skill as "no".

Repeats

Repeating an item versus repeating a skill
SituationRule and consequence
Repeating an item Permitted within the same skill. The last intentional performance is what is scored. You do not need to announce an item repeat, though saying "I would like to repeat that" removes ambiguity. This is generally safe and worth doing for a single item you know you performed poorly, provided you have time.
Repeating a skill You must announce your intention before you begin. The moment you announce it, all previously recorded scores for that skill are erased and you are evaluated as if performing the skill for the first time. That means you must redo every item in the skill, and the time cost is usually prohibitive.
A stopped procedure or skillCannot be repeated.
Where the policy is postedOn the door of each skills room. Read it during observation time.
The repeat trap, stated plainly

The candidate guide contains two explicit cautions, and both come from observed candidate behaviour:

  • Candidates who have not watched the clock and then try to repeat an entire skill for one missed item frequently run out of time, losing far more than the single item they were chasing.
  • Candidates repeating a skill often focus so heavily on the previously missed item that they overlook others, and end with a lower score than they had already earned.

The operational rule: repeat an item freely if you have time. Repeat a skill almost never, and only if you are early in the skill, you have made a fundamental error such as working on the wrong eye or omitting a sequence of items, and you have clearly enough time to complete every item again.

Attempts, interventions, and stops

What happens when things go wrong
EventRule
Attempts per item You are allowed four attempts. On unsuccessfully completing the fourth, the examiner will stop you and instruct you to move to the next skill.
Consequence of a stop Every remaining item in that skill is scored "no", and the skill cannot be repeated. This is why you must not burn four attempts on one difficult quadrant.
Safety stop If you endanger the standardised patient you will be stopped immediately. The SP may also stop you if they are concerned about their own safety.
Intervention If the SP, proctor, or examiner intervenes, corrective action is expected before you proceed. Do not carry on as though nothing happened: acknowledge it, correct it out loud, then continue. For example, if you are asked to wash your hands, wash them and say that you are doing so.
Sequencing Items are listed in the order in which they should optimally be performed, but you may alter the sequence of items within a skill as long as your sequence makes logical sense, and you may alter the order of skills within a station.
Declaring you are finished Saying "I am finished with this station" ends scoring. The examiner leaves, the SP stays and begins preparing the room, and no further items are scored. It is your choice, and there is rarely a good reason to do it. If you make a confusing statement or start casual conversation, you will be reminded that it is your responsibility to state whether you are finished.
Equipment failure Tell the SP, who summons staff. Staff resolve it and instruct you how to proceed, including granting additional time if appropriate. Additional time appears in the top left corner of your device. Non-affected candidates are paused at the end of the cycle so everyone resynchronises.
Injury to you Notify staff immediately. Cover any open wound. Use universal precautions. Any bleeding must be reported and stopped before continuing. No additional time is given for an injury caused by candidate error.

Hygiene and patient safety

Hygiene is a scored item in both stations. Here is how it is lost.
  1. Touching your face or hair after washing your hands. This is named explicitly in the candidate guide as breaking protocol, and you may then be required to wash again before touching the patient or the contact surfaces of the equipment. It is the single commonest hygiene failure, and it happens when candidates push up their glasses or move their hair.
  2. Not washing at all, because you assumed disinfecting the slit lamp was sufficient. Wash to CDC standard during observation time.
  3. Failing to state that you would disinfect the instruments (anterior station item 1).
  4. Touching the eye or lashes with a bottle tip when instilling a drop.
  5. Contaminating the gonioscopy lens or the fundus lens by setting it down on a non-clean surface, or handling the optical face.
  6. Not covering an open wound on a finger or hand.
  7. Reusing a cotton-tipped applicator or a tissue between structures or between uses.

Practical prevention: tie your hair back for the photograph and the exam; push your glasses up before you wash; wash during observation and then keep your hands in your field of view; and if you do touch your face, say so and wash again. Correcting it out loud demonstrates the behaviour the item is assessing.

Patient safety behaviours that are scored or that prevent a stop
BehaviourWhat to do
Warn before every touch"I am going to turn your eyelid out now." "The lens is going to touch your eye now; it will feel like a slight pressure."
Warn before every bright light"This next light is the brightest one and it will be uncomfortable for a few seconds."
Respect personal spaceDo not lean on the patient, do not brace on their face harder than necessary, and do not touch below the shoulder.
No handshakeNot permitted, for hygiene.
Light exposure disciplineDo not hold the beam on one area for an extended period, particularly the fovea. Reduce illumination when you can, and examine the macula last.
Accept blinks and breaksThe SP may blink or ask for a break. Grant it, say something reassuring, and resume.
Instrument controlNever release the gonioscopy lens while it is on the eye; retract the tonometer smoothly; do not advance the slit lamp into the patient.
Drop techniqueDo not touch the eye or lashes with the bottle; instil into the correct eye and say which; ask the patient to close gently rather than squeeze.
Check comfortAsk once or twice during the station: "Are you comfortable? Let me know if you need a break."

Communication and professionalism, which the SP scores

The behaviours that earn the 11% communication and professionalism domain
Domain elementWhat it looks like
Treating the patient with respect Introduce yourself, address them as Mr. or Ms. Lee, explain before you act, ask permission for positioning, acknowledge discomfort, and thank them.
Sharing information effectively Explain in plain language, in a logical order, at a reasonable pace, without jargon, checking understanding.
Receiving information effectively Let the patient finish. Do not interrupt. Reflect back what they said ("So the flashing started three days ago and the shadow came yesterday, is that right?"). Ask open questions before closed ones.
Collaborating with the patient Offer options where they exist, ask about preferences and constraints, and acknowledge the practical impact of what you are proposing.
Collaborating with other professionals Say out loud who you are involving and why: "I am going to telephone the retina service now and speak to them directly, and I will write to your family doctor as well."
Managing your own manner Examiners, proctors, and SPs are trained to be neutral and to show little emotion. Do not read that as disapproval, and do not let it flatten your own manner. Stay warm and steady.
What to do when a station goes badly
  1. Do not abandon the remaining items. Each item is scored independently. A missed quadrant does not affect the next one.
  2. Do not chase the lost item with four attempts. Two attempts, then move on.
  3. Do not announce a skill repeat unless you are early and the error was fundamental.
  4. Keep narrating. Description items are still available even if the technique item was lost.
  5. Reset your manner. The SP scores communication in this station and knows nothing about the previous one.
  6. File an incident report in the break if something outside your control contributed.
  7. Then let it go. There are eleven other stations, one of the ten encounters does not count at all, and the pass decision is made on the whole session.

Chapter recap
  1. Ask about views early, and know that confirmation means only that something is visible.
  2. Nothing may obstruct the left ocular or the central BIO camera, and you may never look at the monitor.
  3. Four attempts then a stop, and a stop scores every remaining item in that skill as "no".
  4. Two attempts is the practical limit; then move on.
  5. Repeat items freely; repeat skills almost never, because announcing it erases everything.
  6. A stopped skill cannot be repeated.
  7. Take visible corrective action after any intervention.
  8. Hygiene is scored: wash during observation, then do not touch your face or hair.
  9. Warn before every touch and every bright light, respect personal space, and never touch below the shoulder.
  10. A bad station is one station. Keep narrating, reset your manner, and move on.
